Race the twins

The determinant and the permanent of a square grid add up the same products; only the signs differ. Press Race: your browser computes both for bigger and bigger random grids of zeros and ones, and times them. On tiny grids the permanent can even be quicker; then it falls behind, doubling its time at every size, while the determinant stays instant.

What am I looking at?

The twins. Pick one cell in every row and every column of an m by m grid and multiply them. There are m! ways. The permanent adds all those products; the determinant gives half of them a minus sign. That sign is what makes the determinant easy: elimination computes it in about m³ steps without dividing anything into fractions (here: the Bareiss method, exact whole numbers). Elimination does divide, exactly; the VP card's claims are about recipes with only + and ×, and the determinant has one of those too, still of polynomial size, as the card says. For the permanent a standard general recipe is Ryser's formula from 1963, about m·2^m steps (here with a Gray code, so each step changes one column). Glynn's formula (2010) has the same order with half as many terms, but it ends by dividing by 2^(m−1), which arithmetic modulo an even number cannot do directly; this page counts modulo an even number so that it also gets the parity, so it uses Ryser's. No recipe of polynomial size is known.

The chart shows the measured time of each, on a scale where every grid line is ten times longer. The determinant's dots hardly rise (small bumps are the browser warming up, not the mathematics). The permanent's climb in a straight line: a fixed factor per size, about 2 and a little more. On very small grids the permanent can win: it has few column sets to add up, and the determinant here works with exact big whole numbers, which cost more per step. Where the lines cross depends on the browser and the machine; the summary under the chart says where they crossed in your run.

Where the twins agree. In arithmetic modulo 2, plus and minus are the same, so the permanent and the determinant are equal there. The page checks this on every grid it races: their parities always match. That is why the claims on the VP card say "wherever 1 + 1 is not 0".

What this is not. A race of two recipes is not a proof that no faster recipe for the permanent exists. That is what the two November claims on the VP card say, for recipes made of + and ×; the VP card explains what has been checked of each.
