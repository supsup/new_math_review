Four pictures before any text

Three collections of machine-checked mathematics landed big claims this autumn. Each picture below makes one of them smaller. Look first; the three sentences under each picture say what you just saw.

Read it left to right. The left columns are things a computer can settle: does the formal statement say what the headline says, and does the proof check. The right columns are things only people can settle: is there a proof a person can read, who wrote the argument, and has a mathematician read it.

The pattern. The biggest claim, the Riemann Hypothesis, is the most machine-checked and the least human-read: a readable guide exists, but it is unfinished. The two results that came from human papers fill the right-hand columns first.

Same headline, different sentence. Two labs each claim "ETH is false" and "VP ≠ VNP", but their formal statements differ, and in both pairs Anthropic's Lean file covers more of its paper than OpenAI's does.

Picture 2

Change the signs and the problem changes

Six ways to pick one cell from every row and column. Multiply the three cells in each picture and add up the six products. That sum is the permanent.

Flip three signs and you get the determinant. The determinant has a recipe of polynomial size, with no division needed. For the permanent nothing like that is known: Ryser's exact recipe from 1963 takes about m·2m operations.

"VP ≠ VNP" says the permanent really is hard for any circuit built from + and ×, however clever, over fields where 1 + 1 is not 0. Both labs claim it. It is the arithmetic cousin of P ≠ NP and does not settle P ≠ NP.

How high is the floor? Every bound written as "m to the power …"

old proven floor (about m²·log m) Anthropic's claimed floor, in Lean OpenAI's claimed floor, paper only ceiling: the 1963 method

A flat line would mean "polynomial". The old floor is flat at about power 2. The two new floors keep rising forever, and that is all "superpolynomial" means.

They rise very slowly. With the unknown constant set to 1, Anthropic's floor does not pass m³ until the grid is about 108 wide, and does not pass m10 until about 10301. The ceiling leaves the chart before m = 100.

So the truth is somewhere in a very tall gap. The claims move the floor from "flat" to "rising". They do not say the permanent needs anything like 2m.

The theorems say "for some constant c > 0 and all large enough m"; nobody states c, so the buttons show how much it matters. Anthropic: more than mc·(log₂ m)^(1/3) gates (vp-vnp/Statement.lean). OpenAI: exp(Ω((log n)^(5/4))) for approximating circuits, stated in the paper, not in its Lean file (lean/docs/379.md). Old floor: Baur and Strassen. Ceiling: Ryser.

  1. x
  2. y
  3. x + y
  4. −1
  5. (−1) · y
  6. x + (−1)·y
  7. (x + y) · (x − y)
size 7  no minus sign: subtract by multiplying with −1

Same computation, different bill. The two labs do not measure circuit size the same way, so their numbers cannot be compared directly.

The bills can never be far apart. Anthropic's count is at most 5 times OpenAI's, plus 1. OpenAI's count is never more than Anthropic's. Here: 3 and 7.

So "small circuit" means the same thing in both. A factor of 5 cannot turn a polynomial into something bigger, so each lab's bare "VP ≠ VNP" follows from the other's.

This is Fixpoint's paper derivation from the two statement files, not a machine check. Three more wording differences needed care: OpenAI's size bounds hold "from some point on" and Anthropic's at every size; OpenAI fixes 2n variables at step n and Anthropic allows any number; OpenAI says the two classes are "not equal" and Anthropic says one sits strictly inside the other. What does not carry over from Anthropic's file to OpenAI's: the growth rate, the approximate-circuit version, and the permanent by name.

Picture 4

A crossing, paid for in four cheaper pieces

bits paid if you cut between the rows bits paid if you cut between the columns the README's promised limit

Two wires cross. Cut the picture in half either way, between the rows or between the columns, and you cut both wires. A crossing is expensive in both directions at once.

Rewrite the crossing as a short sum. Each piece is cheaper than the crossing when you add its two directions together, and the pieces at the ends cost almost nothing in one of them.

That saving is the engine of Anthropic's 3-SAT algorithm. Its one-paragraph summary promises about 10% per round, and the rounds repeat at ever larger scales. Both VP ≠ VNP papers then use the same trade: either such savings exist and you can out-run any small circuit, or they do not and the circuit is stuck.

Fixpoint computed the first three tables by exact elimination over fractions (tools/check_swap_singlet_ranks.py). For four bits, modular elimination (tools/check_swap_ranks_kron.py) and the closed formula certify the rank. All stay under the README's bound of 12 to the power of the wire length. A fresh model, given only the README's paragraph and the definitions, derived the same formula and tables in about six minutes, and showed that 12 cannot be made smaller for this way of splitting (the experiment and its limits). In bits, the limit for long wires is log₂ 12 out of 4 per bit, a ratio of 0.896. The paper itself uses a different expansion and gets 11/12. The last sentence above is Fixpoint's reading of the two proof overviews, not a checked equivalence.
