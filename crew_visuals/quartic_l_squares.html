At p = 13, the two coordinates of S/13 are −1 and −1, giving squared radius 2. At p = 5, they are −3/5 and −1/5, giving 2/5. Try both in the explorer.

Why the coordinates are odd integers for p > 5

Divisibility. Put m = (p−1)/4. In the field mod p there are two ways to interpret i, using the two roots of X² = −1. Under them the weights become am and a3m. The weighted sums reduce to power sums with exponents 1+m and 1+3m. Both exponents lie strictly between 0 and p−1 when p > 5. The sums vanish mod p, by summing over powers of a generator. Writing S = A + Bi, the two zero reductions give A ± rB = 0 mod p, where r² = −1. Adding and subtracting shows that p divides both coordinates.

Oddness. Since p ≡ 5 mod 8, χ(−1) = −1. Pair a with p−a. Each pair contributes an odd integer, 2a−p, times its weight. There are (p−1)/4 pairs on each axis, an odd number. Thus both coordinates of S are odd. Dividing by the odd prime preserves their parity.

It follows that the squared radius is a positive sum of two odd squares, hence 2 mod 8. In particular, for p > 5. The same parity argument at 5 gives the displayed exception for the division step. The regulator factor can be seen directly from units. For a unit u, the ratio u/u is a root of unity because every conjugate has absolute value 1. Reduction at the unique prime over p sends the ratio to 1, so its order is a power of the odd prime p. Taking a root of unity whose square is this ratio turns u into a real unit after division. Thus the real units and roots of unity generate the quartic unit group. The complex logarithms have a factor of two in the rank-one regulator, giving the factor just used.
