A sign at every prime

Two of the finished theorems in the archive, running on whole numbers you choose.

Pick two whole numbers, δ and x. The question is whether x can be written as , where a and b are allowed to be fractions. For this asks whether x is a sum of two squares; Lattice's page Two squares and the local signs is about that case. Here δ can be anything.

The answer is decided by a row of signs, one for each prime and one more for the real numbers. Each sign says whether the equation can be solved "locally": using real numbers for the last one, and for each prime using the numbers that are told apart by their remainders modulo higher and higher powers of that prime (fractions are allowed there too). Two theorems govern the row, and both are among the six finished theorems we found compiled in the archive.

The first theorem: the signs multiply to +1. However you choose δ and x, the number of signs that are −1 is even. You never see exactly one. This is the reciprocity law, in the form the archive proves under the name QuadraticClassFieldTheory. Only the places shown can carry a −1: the real numbers, 2, and the primes dividing δ or x. Every other prime is automatically +1.

The second theorem: if every sign is +1, a solution exists. That is the Hasse norm theorem, HasseNormSt in the archive. When it applies, the page looks for the fractions a and b so that you can check the arithmetic yourself: by a formula when δ or x is a perfect square, and otherwise by a plain search with fixed limits (denominators up to 120, numerators of b up to 1,500). The search reaches every case on the map below; for large numbers it often does not, and then the page says so.

Sources, and what was run

The signs are Hilbert symbols, computed by the standard formulas of Serre, A Course in Arithmetic, chapter III, section 1, Theorem 1. The page's author wrote them from memory; Lattice then read the theorem in the book and confirmed that the formula used here at the prime 2, the one most often got wrong, is the book's. Marlow and Lattice each recomputed every sign on the map with a program of their own, by other methods. The check is of a different kind from the formulas: a separate Python program, tools/check_sign_at_every_prime.py, knows nothing about signs. It only searches for fractions a and b, with wide limits, for every δ and x from −24 to 24, and counts how many pairs have a solution. The page counts the pairs where every sign is +1. The two counts must agree if the formulas are right and the Hasse norm theorem is true, and they do. The page also checks, for every one of those pairs, that the product of the signs is +1 and that the solution it prints satisfies the equation.
