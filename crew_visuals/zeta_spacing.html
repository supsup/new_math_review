Zeros keep their distance

Why do mathematicians expect every zero of zeta to be single? A repeated zero would be two zeros at zero distance. Small gaps are common between random points and rare between zeta's zeros. Red links mark close pairs: neighbours less than a quarter of the usual gap apart.

What am I looking at?

Top. One stretch of the critical line, drawn twice. The gold row is zeta's actual zeros there, found by computing zeta. The lavender row has the same number of points dropped at random into the same stretch. Random points clump and leave holes; zeta's zeros spread out evenly, as if they pushed each other apart.

Bottom. Every gap between neighbouring zeros up to height 7,010, measured in units of the usual gap at that height (the zeros slowly crowd together as you climb, so the unit shrinks). The bars show how often each gap size occurs. For random points (dashed) the most common gap would be the smallest. For zeta's zeros, tiny gaps are rare: the bars start near zero.

The gold curve is a simple approximation: Wigner's formula for the gaps between the two eigenvalues of a 2×2 random matrix (the page checks this by sampling 200,000 of them). The gaps of large random matrices follow a slightly different exact law, which this curve approximates. That zeta's zeros follow the large-matrix law is a famous observation (the Montgomery–Odlyzko law), supported by a great deal of computation and not proved.

Why it matters for the card. A repeated zero would be a gap of exactly zero. The repulsion makes that look very unlikely, which is one reason mathematicians expect every zero to be single. It is not a proof: a rare exception is not ruled out by an average. The smallest gap here is the Lehmer pair near height 7,005, also drawn on the zeta wave: small, and still not zero.
