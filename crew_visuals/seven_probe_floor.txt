At least 51 readings for an eight-tap board of this kind

An n-reading circular board has eight distinct taps, each with coefficient
1. The wiring opens seven directions of ambiguity modulo a prime p, and
any seven direct probes must distinguish them. We also require the same
0–1 board to be invertible over the rationals. No board in this class
has 50 or fewer readings. This is a lower bound on size. The smallest
working size is still unknown here.

The 43-reading example in seven_probes.html explains why rank alone is
insufficient. Its ordinary rank is 43 and its rank modulo 47 is 36.
Seven consecutive probes resolve the ambiguity. Probes at positions
0, 3, 14, 16, 20, 29, 37 leave an entire direction invisible, even if
every mixed reading is kept.

1. Two bounds leave only finitely many primes at each small size

Take a matrix whose seven rows generate the modular kernel. Any seven
columns are independent because any seven probes recover the kernel
element. Put the first seven columns into identity form by row operations
and call the remaining 7 by (n−7) array P. Every entry and every 2 by 2
minor of P is nonzero, by choosing which identity columns to replace.
The ratios on any two rows of P must be distinct across its columns. The
ratios on any two columns must be distinct across its rows. This gives
p−1 ≥ n−7 and p−1 ≥ 7. Sizes below 8 cannot hold eight distinct taps,
and at size 8 every row is all ones, so n ≥ 9 and P has at least two columns.

The integer determinant of the board is divisible by p^7, since seven
rows can be eliminated modulo p by integer row swaps and additions.
It is also divisible by 8, the eigenvalue of the all-ones vector. The
sum of the squared singular values is 8n, counting the ones in the board.
One singular value is 8 because both row and column sums are 8. Applying
the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality to the other n−1 squared
singular values gives the exact necessary condition

    p^14 (n−1)^(n−1) ≤ [8(n−8)]^(n−1).

The prime is at least 8, so it is coprime to 8. Ordinary invertibility
makes the nonzero integer determinant at least 8p^7 in absolute value.

2. A finite search with exact rejection witnesses

Write H(T) for the polynomial with a 1 at each tap exponent. The board
acts by multiplication by H modulo T^n−1. Rotate the eight offsets to
the least binary mask. Distinct translates cannot share more than four
taps. Their difference would have fewer than eight nonzero coordinates,
and would restrict a set of seven or fewer coordinates of the kernel.
An invertible board has distinct rows, so this difference is nonzero.

For each prime passing the first two bounds, factor T^n−1 modulo p. If
p = n, the polynomial reduces to (T−1)^p and H(1) = 8 rules out any rank
drop. The remaining candidates have p not dividing n, so the factors are
distinct. A degree-seven greatest common divisor can only use factors
of degree 1 through 7. That gives a fast exact modular filter. The replay
checks the full modular gcd and the rational gcd on every survivor.

Only 138 rotated shapes in the search pass both rank tests. The six of
length 43 and the 132 of length 46 all use p = 47. For each one, let g be
the degree-seven modular gcd. The kernel has basis H0, T·H0, …, T^6·H0
with H0 = (T^n−1)/g. The replay finds and checks seven probe positions
whose minor in this basis is zero. Its choice of candidate probe sets
uses a fixed seed. A displayed zero determinant is exact, and the replay
fails if it cannot exhibit a witness for any surviving shape.

    python3 -B tools/check_eight_tap_minimum.py
    python3 -B tools/check_eight_tap_minimum.py 43 46 --show-witnesses

The replay needs python-flint and a C++20 compiler. It uses temporary
files for the scan, then removes them.

See seven_loss_mixer.txt for two working boards and the proof that eight
taps are necessary for this rank-drop construction.
