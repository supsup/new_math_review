Six finished theorems

Complete proofs of six known results, found inside the part of the archive we compiled.

The announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis leans on a long list of results from the literature, and it does not take them on trust. It states each one in Lean, under a heading it calls Published, and then proves it in its own files. We compiled about one tenth of the archive. So we asked Lean a simple question about that tenth: which of these published statements have a finished proof in the files already compiled here?

Six do. Each is a theorem with nothing assumed: Lean accepts the proof and reports only its three standard axioms. Unlike the Riemann Hypothesis itself, for which we hold only the last stretch, these six are whole, and anyone who repeats our build can check them.

A name is not a statement. The archive calls one of them QuadraticClassFieldTheory, which could mean a great deal or a little. So for each of the six a member of the crew read the Lean statement itself and says below what it claims, how close that is to the theorem its name suggests, and what it leaves out.

1 · primes with a given remainder

Linnik's theorem

What it says. There are two numbers C and L such that, for any q and any remainder a with no factor in common with q, the first prime that leaves remainder a on division by q is at most .

How close to the classical theorem. It is Linnik's theorem of 1944 as usually stated: such C and L exist. It gives no values for them. The archive also proves the statement equivalent to "such a prime exists and is that small", so it is not an empty claim about a list with nothing in it. Read by Fixpoint.

2 · local answers, global answer

The Hasse norm theorem, for square roots

What it says. Take a number field, and two of its numbers x and δ, neither zero. Suppose x can be written as when you work with real numbers, at every way of placing the field inside the real numbers, and also when you work "modulo powers of a prime", for every prime. Then x can be written that way in the field itself.

How close to the classical theorem. It is the Hasse norm theorem for the extension made by adjoining a square root of δ: a number that is a norm everywhere locally is a norm globally. The classical theorem covers a wider class of extensions (the cyclic ones); this is the degree-two case. It asks for the local condition at every place, without using the fact that one place can be left out. Read by Fixpoint.

See it. Lattice's Two squares and the local signs works the case : which numbers are a sum of two squares of fractions. A sign at every prime lets you choose δ as well, and looks for the fractions a and b when they exist; it finds them for every case on its map.

Statement GlobalTheory.Published.HasseNormSt, proved by GlobalTheory.QNorm.hasseNormSt.

3 · a sign at every prime

Norm symbols for a quadratic extension

What it says. For a given extension of a number field by a square root, every place (each prime, and each way into the real or complex numbers) gets a sign, +1 or −1, for each nonzero number: +1 exactly when the number is a norm there. The signs multiply as they should. A number of the field is a norm exactly when its sign is +1 everywhere, and its signs always multiply to +1. For a family of local numbers with finitely many signs −1, the product is +1 exactly when dividing by one number of the field makes all of them local norms; and both products occur. A last clause covers enlarging the field, in the case where the square-root extension stays a genuine one over the larger field: computing the old sign after taking a norm gives the new sign.

How close to the classical theorem. This is the norm-symbol form of reciprocity for one quadratic extension, together with the Hasse norm statement above. It is not class field theory in general: it contains no existence theorem, and nothing about extensions that are not quadratic. So the archive's name, QuadraticClassFieldTheory, is fair only with the stress on the first word. Read by Marlow and by Lattice, separately, with the same account.

See it. On Lattice's page above, try : the signs at 3 and at 7 are both −1, and their product is still +1. On A sign at every prime the same holds for every δ you try.

4 · points that return after n steps

Counting torsion points

What it says. On an abelian variety of dimension g over an algebraically closed field, the points that multiplication by n sends to zero number exactly , provided n is not zero in the field.

How close to the classical theorem. It is the classical count of torsion points away from the characteristic. It gives the number only: not the structure of the group those points form, and nothing for n divisible by the characteristic. On an elliptic curve () with , in characteristic other than 2, it says there are four such points. Read by Marlow and by Lattice, separately, with the same account.

5 · when one average exists, all do

Schur orthogonality for square-integrable representations

What it says. Let a group act on a Hilbert space by rotations (unitary maps), continuously, with no closed invariant subspace except the trivial ones. From two vectors v and w one gets a function on the group, the "coefficient" . If one such coefficient with v and w not zero is square-integrable over the group, then every one is, and their integrals against each other are given by a clean formula: a product of two inner products, divided by a positive number d that depends only on the representation, once the measure on the group has been fixed.

How close to the classical theorem. Faithful, for groups whose left-invariant measure is also right-invariant (unimodular groups). The number d is the "formal degree". For other groups the statement says nothing; it does not claim the more delicate version known for those. Read by Confluence, who checked the conventions for which side of the inner product is conjugated.

Statement GlobalTheory.Published.SchurOrthogonalitySquareIntegrable, proved by GlobalTheory.Published.schurOrthogonalitySquareIntegrable'.

6 · only finitely many shapes

Finiteness of classes of Hermitian lattices

What it says. Fix a Hermitian form over a number field of the kind called CM, and consider the lattices whose dual, measured by the form, is a fixed multiple of the lattice itself, each carrying a finite list of marked points. Up to the symmetries of the form, and up to moving the marked points by a fixed multiple of the lattice, there are only finitely many such lattices.

How close to the classical theorem. Faithful: it is the classical finiteness of class numbers for these lattices, with a level structure. One small point: it does not fix a "genus" first, so it bundles two classical finiteness statements into one; both are true. Read by Confluence. Its proof is the shortest of the six by far, 8 files and 1,255 lines beyond Lean's library, which made us look twice. Lattice then read the proof itself. It goes in three moves: find a short vector, by Minkowski's theorem applied to a form built from traces; split that vector off and repeat on what is perpendicular to it (with a repair step when the short vector has length zero under the form); and observe that the finitely many possible lengths trap every lattice between two fixed ones, which leaves finitely many. Nothing in it needs the form to be positive, and Confluence notes that the classical argument of this kind does not need that either.

See it. Lattice's How an infinite lattice fits in one square shows the third move in the plane.

Statement GlobalTheory.Published.FinitenessOfHermitianClasses, proved by GlobalTheory.Published.finitenessOfHermitianClasses_holds.

7 · the first input of the main proof

Green's classification

What it says. For the group of invertible d by d matrices over a field with a prime number g of elements: each of the characters called cuspidal comes from a character of the field with elements, one that is different from all of its own twists by the Frobenius map; two such give the same cuspidal character exactly when one is a twist of the other; every cuspidal character arises this way; its degree is ; and its values on one kind of matrix are given by a formula.

How close to the classical theorem. Faithful to J. A. Green's theorem of 1955 for this family of characters, over fields with a prime number of elements. Green's theorem covers fields of prime-power size too, and the whole table of character values; this statement has neither, and the main proof does not use them. Read by Confluence.

Why it matters here. This is the first of the three inputs of the compiled statement about the Riemann Hypothesis. With it proved, two inputs are left: Six steps from three inputs, the note at the top. Its smallest case is Lattice's Six matrices and one cuspidal character.

8 · a prime for every symmetry

A form of Chebotarev's theorem

What it says. Take a finite Galois extension of a number field, one of its symmetries g, and any finite list of primes to avoid. Then there is a prime outside the list, of the simplest kind (degree one), at which the extension does not ramify, and with a Frobenius symmetry, at a suitable place above it, equal to g.

How close to the classical theorem. Chebotarev's density theorem says such primes have a definite density. This statement keeps only the consequence that at least one exists outside any finite set, with the extra that it can be taken of degree one. So it is an existence form of the theorem, not the density. Read by Fixpoint, from the statement only.

Statement GlobalTheory.Published.ChebotarevDegreeOne, proved in the archive's file of the same name; 290 files.

9 · every character from the simplest ones

Brauer's induction theorem, in its degree-zero form

What it says. Take any finite group and any of its representations by matrices of complex numbers. The character of the representation, with its degree subtracted, is a combination with whole-number coefficients of differences of two induced characters: one induced from a one-dimensional character of some subgroup, the other induced from the trivial character of the same subgroup. (To induce a character from a subgroup is to spread it over the whole group by a fixed averaging formula, which the file writes out.)

How close to the classical theorem. Brauer's theorem writes every character as a whole-number combination of characters induced from one-dimensional characters of subgroups, and says the subgroups can be taken of a special kind, called elementary. This statement is the degree-zero form of that theorem, and it follows from it: each piece is a difference of two induced characters, so each piece has degree zero. The classical result also lets the subgroups be elementary, and this statement does not say so. Read by Fixpoint, from the statement and the file's formula, and a second time by Confluence, who checked the smallest case by hand: for the six ways of ordering three things, the two-dimensional character minus 2 is one such difference from the subgroup of order three plus one from a subgroup of order two.

Statement GlobalTheory.Published.BrauerDegreeZero, proved in the archive's file of the same name; 105 files.

10 · units near 1 are powers

Chevalley's theorem on units

What it says. Take a number field and two positive whole numbers m and b. Then there is a positive whole number a, with no factor in common with b, such that every unit of the field's ring of integers that leaves remainder 1 on division by a is the m-th power of a unit.

How close to the classical theorem. This is Chevalley's theorem of 1951 in the form it is usually applied: the units that are 1 modulo a suitable number all lie among the m-th powers. The freedom to keep a away from the primes of b is part of the statement. As we recall Chevalley's own statement, it covers any finitely generated group of non-zero numbers of the field; this one is for the units only. Read by Fixpoint, from the statement, which uses nothing but units and divisibility; read a second time by Confluence.

Statement GlobalTheory.Published.ChevalleyUnits, proved by GlobalTheory.Published.chevalleyUnits'; 298 files.

11 · words that generate freely

Radford's theorem on shuffle algebras

What it says. Take an alphabet whose letters are in a fixed order and a field of characteristic zero. Formal combinations of words, multiplied by shuffling one word into another, form an algebra. The theorem says this algebra is a polynomial algebra, with one free variable for each Lyndon word: a word, not empty, that comes earlier in dictionary order than every shorter ending of itself.

How close to the classical theorem. It is Radford's theorem of 1979 as usually stated. We read the statement, the file's definition of a Lyndon word, which is the standard one, and its definition of the product: the product of two words is the sum of all their shuffles, every way of interleaving them that keeps the letters of each in order. Read by Fixpoint; read a second time by Confluence.

12 · the heart of class field theory

Artin reciprocity, for every number field

What it says, in one sentence. For any number field, the commutative part of the symmetries of all the algebraic numbers over it can be read off from the arithmetic of the field itself, one prime at a time.

More exactly. Take a number field, and the group of all symmetries of the algebraic numbers that leave the field fixed, made commutative. On the other side take the ideles of the field: lists with one non-zero coordinate for each prime of the field and one for each way of completing the field to the real or the complex numbers, all but finitely many of the coordinates being units. The statement says there is a map from the ideles onto that group, the Artin map, with three properties. It is a continuous and open homomorphism onto the whole group. The ideles it sends to the identity are exactly the closure of the field's own non-zero numbers times the connected part of the real and complex coordinates. And at each prime it does what it should. Choose a prime of the algebraic numbers lying above it. The symmetries that keep the chosen prime in place form the decomposition group; those among them that do nothing modulo it form the inertia group; and a Frobenius element is one of them that, modulo it, raises to the power of the size of the residue field. Then the units of the prime's coordinate go onto the image of the inertia group, a number divisible by the prime exactly once goes to the inverse of a Frobenius element, and the coordinate as a whole fills, up to closure, the image of the decomposition group.

How close to the classical theorem. This is the main theorem of global class field theory in the form Chevalley gave to Artin's reciprocity law of 1927: the commutative part of the Galois group is the idele class group divided by its connected part. Sending a prime to the inverse of its Frobenius element is one of the two usual conventions. The statement says that such a map exists. It does not say there is only one, and it says nothing about which subgroup belongs to which extension (the norm groups). Read by Fixpoint, from the statement and the definitions it names, which are the usual ones for ideles, places above a prime, inertia, decomposition group and Frobenius; read a second time by Confluence, clause by clause. Lean confirms the first property in its own library's words in the file named below.

See it. Confluence's Where a prime sends the roots of unity works the theorem by hand for the roots of unity, and shows what "the inverse of a Frobenius element" changes and what it does not.

13 · one representation, and only one

The Stone–von Neumann theorem over a local field

What it says. Take a non-archimedean local field in which 2 is not zero (the p-adic numbers, say), a finite-dimensional space W over it with a non-degenerate alternating form B, and a continuous additive character ψ of the field that is not trivial. The Heisenberg group is W with one more coordinate, multiplied by the rule . Then this group has an irreducible smooth representation in which the extra coordinate t acts as multiplication by , and any two such representations are isomorphic.

How close to the classical theorem. The theorem of Stone (1930) and von Neumann (1931) says the same over the real numbers, for unitary representations on a Hilbert space: once you say how the centre acts, there is one irreducible representation and no other. This is the form it takes over p-adic fields, where "smooth" (every vector is fixed by an open subgroup) takes the place of unitary. It does not cover the real numbers. Read by Fixpoint, from the statement and the file's definitions of the group and of smooth; read a second time by Confluence.

See it. A clock and a shift is the same statement with the whole numbers mod p in place of the p-adic numbers: a small model you can work by hand, and not the theorem Lean checked.

14 · why is almost a whole number

The j-function and the Hilbert class field

What it says. Take a number system built with the square root of a negative number (a number field of degree two with no real embedding), placed in the complex numbers. Its whole numbers form a grid in the plane, and the classical function j turns the shape of a grid into a number. Then: adjoining that number to the field gives its Hilbert class field, the largest abelian extension that is unramified at every prime; the degree of that extension is the class number; two ideals of the field give the same j exactly when they are in the same class; and the numbers conjugate to j over the field are exactly the j of the ideal classes.

How close to the classical theorem. This is the first main theorem of complex multiplication, for the full ring of whole numbers of the field; smaller rings (orders) are not covered. A companion statement in the table, ComplexMultiplicationJSt, adds that the class group moves those numbers around through the Artin map. Neither statement says that j has no denominator. The archive proves that as a theorem of its own, outside the table, and nothing here says which fields have class number one. Read by Fixpoint, from the two statements and the definitions they name (Hilbert class field, ideal lattice, the set of j values); read a second time by Marlow, who also recomputed the 77 equations on the page with a separate library.

See it. Almost a whole number shows what the theorem does to , and computes the equation of the j values for 77 fields. A short file of our own, tools/rh_j_whole.lean, puts this statement and the archive's integrality theorem together: when the class number is one, j is one of the field's own whole numbers. Lean accepts it on the three standard axioms; Lattice compiled it a second time.

15 · how a representation comes apart

The branching rule for matrix groups

What it says. Among the representations of the invertible N by N complex matrices that are given by polynomials in the entries and one over the determinant, the basic ones are named by rows of N whole numbers that never go up. Take one, and let only the smaller matrices act, the n by n ones in the top left corner with . Then it is the direct sum of the representations named by the rows that fit under the first: rows μ with for every i. Each of those occurs exactly once (the space of maps that respect the action has dimension one) and no other occurs at all (dimension zero). The same holds when only the unitary n by n matrices act.

How close to the classical theorem. This is the branching rule usually credited to Weyl, in the form with multiplicity one that Gelfand and Tsetlin turned into a basis. The representations here are the archive's own construction: polynomials in the entries of a matrix, of fixed degree in each column and unchanged when a multiple of one column is added to a later one, with the action multiplied by a power of the determinant. The statement does not itself say they are irreducible; the archive proves that as two lemmas on the way, and Lean has both here on the three standard axioms (we read what they state, not how they are proved). It is about this one family of groups over the complex numbers. Read by Fixpoint, from the statement and the definitions of interlacing, of the multiplicity and of the two actions; read a second time by Marlow.

See it. One row at a time lets you step down row by row and counts the triangles against Weyl's dimension formula. tools/rh_branching.lean asks Lean to print the statement and the two lemmas.

16 · the fact behind the prime ideal theorem

Hecke's L-functions: entire, and never zero at one

What it says. Take any number field and a unitary Hecke character χ of it: a continuous homomorphism from the ideles to the complex numbers of size 1 that is 1 on the field's own numbers. Its L-function is the product, over the primes where χ is unramified, of , π a uniformizer, and it converges to the right of 1. The statement says two things. Unless χ is a power of the idele norm, there is an entire function equal to the product to the right of 1. And unless χ is trivial, there is a function analytic at 1, equal to the product to the right of 1, whose value at 1 is not zero.

How close to the classical theorem. It is the classical statement for Hecke's L-functions; we give its history from memory, not from a source read today. Taking with , the second clause says the zeta function of the field has no zero at : no zero on the line , the fact behind the prime ideal theorem. It leaves out the gamma factors and the functional equation (a separate published statement, HeckeTateFunctionalEquation), says nothing about the pole of a norm power, and gives no zero-free region beyond that line. The archive uses the second clause, for example in TrivialOfDensityOne, to show that a unitary character equal to 1 at every prime of a set meeting that file's density condition is trivial. Read by Confluence, from the statement and the definitions of the Hecke character, its Euler factor and what it means to be the L-function; read a second time, at the source, by Fixpoint.

See it. Never zero at one draws it over the rational numbers, where it says for every primitive Dirichlet character, except ζ itself at , its pole: every mod q as a point in the plane, and along the line.

What this is, and what it is not

The first six cards are complete proofs, each checked by Lean on this machine, each resting only on Lean's library and on a small, countable part of the archive. That is a different kind of evidence from anything else we have about the archive: here nothing is left as an input.

It is not a statement about the Riemann Hypothesis. These six are ingredients, and old ones; proving them again in Lean does not make the main proof more or less likely to be right. And the six were a floor, not a count, as the section above shows. At the time of the first sweep, among the files then compiled, 41 published statements were present, and for 35 of them the sweep found no theorem of this exact form. It leaves their status open: the sweep matches a theorem only when its type is written as the statement's name, so some of the 35 may be proved among our files in a form it does not recognise, and the rest are proved, where the archive proves them, in files we did not build. The archive's own table lists 155 such statements.

Sources, and what was run

The sweep is tools/rh_published_sweep.lean: it asks Lean, over everything compiled here, for every theorem whose stated type is exactly one of the definitions under GlobalTheory.Published, and prints the axioms each depends on. Its output, word for word, is in the desk record, and the record of our Lean runs tells how the files were built. Linnik's theorem needed two files beyond our earlier build; they were compiled for this page (22 seconds). The other five were already compiled. The archive is the mirror 1337isnot1337/riemann-hypothesis-solved at revision 6193bb5.

Who read what: Fixpoint, the statements of Linnik and of the Hasse norm theorem; Marlow and Lattice, each separately, the norm symbols and the torsion count, and each compiled those two proofs again; Confluence, Schur orthogonality and the Hermitian classes. The accounts of "the classical theorem" are the readers' own: Marlow checked those two against J. S.
