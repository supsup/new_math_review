Where a prime sends the roots of unity

The n-th roots of unity are the n points ζ, ζ², …, ζⁿ = 1 spaced evenly round a circle, with ζ = e2πi/n. The ones that are not roots of unity of any smaller order are the ζa with a sharing no factor with n; there are φ(n) of them. The field they make, ℚ(ζ), has exactly φ(n) symmetries: for each such a, the symmetry σa sends ζ to ζa. Composing two of them multiplies the exponents mod n, so the symmetries form the group of units mod n.

Artin reciprocity, for this one field, says which symmetry belongs to a prime p that does not divide n: its Frobenius is σp, "raise to the p-th power". Everything about how p behaves in ℚ(ζ) follows from the residue of p mod n. In particular, p breaks up into φ(n)/f primes, each of degree f, where f is the order of p mod n. The archive proves Artin reciprocity for every number field (card 12); this page is the case where everything can be computed by hand.

2 · Every prime gets a symmetry, and every symmetry gets its share

Drop each prime below a million into its class mod n. Chebotarev's theorem, which for this field is Dirichlet's theorem on primes in progressions, says each of the φ(n) classes gets the same share in the long run. The archive's Chebotarev card proves the existence form: for any symmetry, a prime outside any finite list with that Frobenius. The bars show how far each class is from an even share. The gold bar is the class of 1, the primes that split completely. The teal bar is the class of your p.

3 · "The inverse of a Frobenius element"

The card states reciprocity with ideles, and in the archive's normalization a number divisible by the prime exactly once goes to the inverse of a Frobenius element. For ℚ(ζ), the idele that is p at the place p and 1 everywhere else goes to σp−1 = σp′, where p′ is the inverse of p mod n. That is, ζ goes to ζp′, not to ζp. The classical statement, with ideals, sends p to σp itself. The two maps differ only by inverting every symmetry, a choice of convention, and nothing on this page depends on it. p and p′ have the same order mod n, so the cycles in the picture have the same lengths and p splits the same way. Tick the box above to see the arrows reversed.

The other two clauses of the card can also be seen here. If p divides n, write n = pkm with m not divisible by p. The units of the p-adic coordinate fill the inertia group, the symmetries σa with a ≡ 1 mod m, and there are φ(pk) of them. That number is the e in the factorization: Φn ≡ Φme mod p, each prime above p appearing e times. And the whole p-adic coordinate fills the decomposition group, the symmetries σa with a a power of p mod m, of size e·f. Try n = 12, p = 3 and n = 27, p = 3.

Sources, and how this was computed

The statement is the archive's, as on card 12 of Six finished theorems, and tools/rh_artin.lean prints it. The cyclotomic case is classical: Dedekind and Kummer give the factorization of p in ℤ[ζ] from the factorization of the cyclotomic polynomial Φn mod p.

The page computes Φn by dividing xⁿ − 1 by Φd for the proper divisors d of n. It factors Φn mod p by distinct-degree factorization (gcd with xpd − x), using exact integers throughout. The prime counts come from a sieve to 10⁶ in the browser. tools/check_artin_map.py runs this page's own arithmetic in node over a grid of n and p. It compares the factor counts with an independent count by Berlekamp's method: the number of irreducible factors is the dimension of the space of polynomials that the p-th power map fixes modulo Φn, found by Gaussian elimination mod p. It also recounts the primes quoted in the self-check.
