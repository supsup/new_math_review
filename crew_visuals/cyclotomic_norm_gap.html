Prime splitting and the norm of 139

In a root-of-unity field, prime splitting tells us how many copies of a prime a norm must contain. Since 139 is 1 modulo 46, it splits into degree-one primes in the field of 46th roots of unity. The prime test permits a single factor of 139. A quadratic subfield shows why 139 still cannot be the norm of an algebraic integer there.

Can the quadratic subfield produce 139?

A norm is a product over conjugates. If an algebraic integer in ℚ(ζ₄₆) has norm n, taking its norm down to ℚ(√−23) first gives an algebraic integer with that same final norm. Every integer in the smaller field has the form a + bω, with a,b ordinary integers and ω = (1 + √−23)/2. Its norm is a² + ab + 6b². Multiply by four to get the square test below.

For 139, |b| can only be 0 through 4. The required squares are 556, 533, 464, 349 and 188, and each falls between two consecutive squares. The same check excludes 47. Their product works in the smaller field, since 45² + 23·14² = 47·139.

Where the extra factor of 47 goes

This example comes from an actual class number. For conductor 47 the relative class number of ℚ(ζ₄₇) is 695. Its imaginary quadratic subfield ℚ(√−47) has class number 5. Dividing isolates the contribution from characters of exact order 46 and gives 139.

One prime ideal over 47 is in the denominator of S/(2·47), and one prime ideal over 139 is in the numerator. They are in the same ideal class because their quotient is generated by that one element. Multiplying the norm by 47 clears the denominator and leaves the norm of the ideal over 139. The square test proves this ideal has no generator.

Read the two prime ideals

Write ζ = ζ₄₆ and B = S/2. Reducing B at the primitive 46th roots in the field mod 47 gives zero everywhere except at ζ = 19. Reducing it at the primitive 46th roots mod 139 gives a single zero, at ζ = 60. The norm 139/47 then gives the fractional ideal identity (B/47) = (139, ζ−60)/(47, ζ−19).

Why a conductor correction can change the kind of norm

Let h be an even character order with an odd factor greater than 1, and let the conductor p be prime with (p−1)/h odd. Pairing the terms at a and p−a gives an odd number of odd coefficients for each of the first h/2 character values. Modulo 2, S equals their sum. The sum of the first h/2 powers of ζh is 2/(1−ζh), and 1−ζh is a unit because h has at least two distinct prime factors. Thus S is even in ℤ[ζh].

At a prime ideal over p, reduce ζh to the corresponding root in the field mod p. This changes χ(a) into a raised to a multiple of (p−1)/h. The sum of these powers times a vanishes unless their exponent is a multiple of p−1. If p > h+1, every reduction vanishes and S/(2p) is integral. If p = h+1, the inverse generator is the unique exceptional root, giving a single denominator prime.

Isolating exact order h in the relative class number formula cancels the common factor 2 from the roots of unity. When p = h+1, the full cyclotomic field has 2p roots of unity and an extra factor p survives. The corrected quotient is the norm of the integral ideal obtained by multiplying (S/(2p)) by its denominator prime. That ideal retains the ideal class of the denominator prime. Try a field where the prime test decides

In ℚ(ζ₁₂), a rational prime congruent to 1 modulo 12 can occur to any exponent. Every other prime needs an even exponent, including 2 and 3. This rule is exact here. The field has class number one, so every ideal is generated by an element.

Why the exponent rule is exact for order 12

For a prime q coprime to 12, the residue degree is the order of q modulo 12. It is 1 for the class of 1 and 2 for classes 5, 7 and 11. For 2, strip its power out of 12 and take the order of 2 modulo 3, which is 2. For 3, the order of 3 modulo 4 is again 2.

The field has discriminant 144 and two complex pairs. The Minkowski bound is 18/π² < 2, so every ideal class has a representative of norm 1. A prime ideal of residue degree f has norm qf.
