Why the poles add

The Lean proof estimates one weighted sum for a whole family of Dirichlet L-functions. That sum can reveal a zero of any member because the contributions at the same pole all point in one direction. Try changing the multiplicities.

A zero of order m gives a term m/(s − ρ) in the logarithmic derivative. The prime-power smoothing contributes the same factor −1/[ρ(ρ − 1)] for every member. With positive weights, any zero gives a nonzero contribution along this common direction. Switch to −1 to see what subtraction could hide.

How the Lean proof uses this

The bound is on a sum of real parts. Lean writes a real part as the average of the character term and its conjugate-character term, then takes the Mellin transform. For a given zero ρ it adds their orders of vanishing with positive weights. The total is called S in poleData. Lean proves S > 0 by finding a member that vanishes and using nonnegativity of all the other orders. Multiplying by −1/[ρ(ρ − 1)] preserves a nonzero residue. The toy uses two contributions and absorbs the factor 1/2 into their weights. The positive sum in Lean

For a real zero strictly between 0 and 1, the common factor is positive. That sign feeds the real-pole case of the window criterion. For a nonreal zero, a suitable complex center makes the nearest pole dominate the Taylor coefficients. In both cases, the upper bound on the prime-power sum makes the pole impossible.
