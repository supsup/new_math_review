Every zero is a crossing

The Riemann zeta function, computed live in your browser on the line where all its nontrivial zeros lie. Left: zeta as a point moving in the plane; it passes through the centre at every zero. Right: the same motion unwound into a wave; every zero is a crossing.

What am I looking at?

Zeta takes a height t and gives back a point in the plane. As t climbs, that point draws the curve. Each time the curve passes through the centre, zeta is zero there.

The wave. Turn that point by the right angle at each height and it always lands on one straight line. How far along that line it sits, ahead or behind, is the wave (called Hardy's Z). Same zeros, easier to see. The dashed segment from the centre and the height of the dot on the wave are the same length.

Single or repeated. At a single zero the wave cuts through with a slope: the short gold tick. A repeated zero would sit flat at zero. Every crossing this page finds has a clearly nonzero estimated slope (a numerical picture: a sign change alone could also be a triple zero). It is proved that, counting far enough up, at least 67% of the zeros counted are single (Alpöge and Furman, 2026), and that every nontrivial zero lies on this line (the Riemann Hypothesis, proved in 2026 and checked by computer; a write-up for people to read is still to come). Whether every zero is single is still open.

A near miss. Sometimes two zeros come very close, and the wave only just gets across between them (a "Lehmer pair"). That is the nearest thing to a repeated zero, and it is still two crossings: a close-up appears whenever one is on screen. The button jumps to the closest pair the page finds below height 7,010.

The counter compares the zeros found so far with a separate formula for how many there should be, θ(t)/π + 1. They stay within 1 of each other: a quick sign that no zero has been missed. This is a picture, not a proof.

Hear it plays the wave as sound, height 10 to 20,000 in ten seconds. Every zero is a moment the sound crosses silence; the zeros crowd closer as it climbs, so the pitch rises.
