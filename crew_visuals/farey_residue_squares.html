After k turns, the reduced fractions with denominator q contribute the integer Ramanujan sum cq(k). Put it in the bucket for q modulo the chosen modulus. Keep only remainders coprime to that modulus. At one turn, the contribution is simply the Möbius number μ(q). The circle and Ramanujan sums.

Choose modulo 5. At N = 2 and one turn, the buckets are 1, −1, 0, 0. Their total vanishes and their spread is 8. At N = 179 and two turns, the buckets are −5, −3, −3, −4. The square of their total is 225, and their spread is 11. Click Subtract the average to see each bucket’s difference from the average.

Characters are the Fourier basis on the reduced remainders. When you expand the sum of the squared character magnitudes, cross terms between distinct buckets disappear by orthogonality. Equal remainders contribute h, giving hΣAr² again. The principal character gives T. All the other squared magnitudes therefore add up to the spread. Modulo 5 and modulo 8 each have four reduced remainders, so the same scale applies to both. Modulo 5 gives one real nonprincipal sum and a complex conjugate pair. Modulo 8 gives three real nonprincipal sums. Exact replay.

Which Riemann hypotheses the two terms test

Fix the modulus and the turn count k. For every ε > 0, the full sum of squares grows at most as N1+ε exactly when GRH holds for every character of that modulus. The same bound for the spread corresponds to the nonprincipal characters. The bound for the square of the total corresponds to ordinary RH, since the principal character’s L-function differs from ζ only by finitely many Euler factors.

At k = 1 this follows from the twisted Möbius growth criterion. For general fixed k, the Dirichlet series of a character’s contributions is with the whole divisor sum in the numerator. Its prime-power factors are geometric sums in χ(p)p1−s. For a prime dividing the modulus the factor is 1. Any zeros of the other factors lie on Re s = 1, so they leave poles from off-line zeros in the open strip. The finite divisor sum transfers the growth bound in the other direction. Squaring the magnitudes doubles the exponent, and the sum has finitely many nonnegative terms. Garg and Maji, equation (4.22) · The fixed-harmonic argument.
