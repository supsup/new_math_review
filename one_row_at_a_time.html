One row at a time

Look at a large symmetric object through a smaller group and it falls apart into pieces. For the groups of invertible matrices there is an exact rule for which pieces you get, and the whole rule is one picture: each new number sits between two old ones. The archive of the announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis proves the rule in Lean, and Lean has checked it on our own machine.

two to six whole numbers that never go up, the first at most 9 more than the last

What you are looking at


    

  1. A row of numbers names a building block. The invertible N by N matrices can act on many spaces besides the one they were born on. Among the ways of acting that are given by polynomials in the entries of the matrix and one over its determinant, the basic ones, from which all the others are built, are named by a row of N whole numbers that never goes up, like 4 2 0. Call the block with that name by its row.
    2. 

  2. Now forget one coordinate. Inside the N by N matrices sit the smaller ones, N − 1 by N − 1, which leave the last coordinate alone. Seen only through them, a block need no longer be basic. It can split into smaller blocks.
    3. 

  3. The rule for the pieces. You get one piece for every shorter row that fits under the old one: its first number between the first and second of the old row, its second between the second and third, and so on. Every such row turns up exactly once, and nothing else turns up. For 4 2 0 that is nine pieces, from 4 2 down to 2 0.
    4. 

  4. Keep going and you get a triangle. Forget another coordinate, and another, until a single number is left. Each way of going all the way down is a triangle of numbers, and the triangles label a full set of independent directions in the space the block acts on. So the number of triangles is the size of the block: its dimension. A formula of Weyl gives that dimension directly, and the page counts both ways.
    5. 




What the Lean statement says, read at the source


The archive names the statement GlobalTheory.Published.WeylBranching. We read its definition and the ones it uses. It holds for every n from 1 up, with N = n + 1, and every row λ of N whole numbers that never goes up (negative numbers allowed).




Interlace h lam muThe row μ fits under λ: λi ≥ μi ≥ λi+1 for every i. This is the picture above.


Nonempty ((sumRep …).Equiv (resRep …))The block for λ, acted on only by the n by n matrices in the top left corner, is the same representation as the direct sum of the blocks for all the rows μ that fit under λ.


Interlace h lam mu → mult … = 1The maps from the block of μ into it that respect the action form a space of dimension exactly one when μ fits under λ: each piece once.


¬ Interlace h lam mu → mult … = 0And of dimension zero when it does not: nothing else.


BranchesAlong (unitaryIncl n) h lamAll of this again when only the unitary n by n matrices act.




What it leaves out, and what stands beside it. The blocks are the archive's own construction: the polynomials in the entries of an N by N matrix that have a fixed degree in each column and do not change when a multiple of one column is added to a later one. A matrix acts on them by a substitution, multiplied by a power of its determinant (the power is the last number of the row), which is how negative numbers are allowed. A separate statement in the archive gives these spaces a basis indexed by tableaux; we read that statement and not its proof. The statement on this page does not itself say that a block is irreducible, that it cannot be split while all the matrices act. The archive proves that as two lemmas on the way, for the whole group and for the unitary group, and Lean has both here on the three standard axioms; we read what they state and not how they are proved. Everything is over the complex numbers and for this one family of groups: the rules for orthogonal and symplectic groups are different theorems.





Sources, and what was run




Every number on this page is computed by the page, in whole numbers. A separate program, tools/check_one_row_at_a_time.py, writes out every candidate triangle under 238 top rows of two to six numbers, keeps the ones whose numbers do sit between their neighbours, and compares the count with a different dimension formula (hooks and contents of the boxes of a diagram). The page's own arithmetic agrees with it on every one of those rows; on load the page checks itself against a sample of twenty. Rows too large to write out, like 9 7 5 3 1 0, are compared with the second formula only.

Reported from Wikipedia, read on 25 November 2026, not from the original works: the rule from U(N) to U(N − 1) with exactly these inequalities, credited to Weyl, The Classical Groups (1946), and described as having multiplicity one ("Restricted representation"); the triangles are named for Gelfand and Tsetlin, who used them in the 1950s to give a basis ("Restricted representation", "Gelfand–Zeitlin integrable system"). The dimension formula is Weyl's, in the form it takes for these groups; the Wikipedia article "Weyl character formula" gives the general form and the case of three numbers, (m₁ + 1)(m₂ + 1)(m₁ + m₂ + 2)/2, which this page checks against its own counts.
