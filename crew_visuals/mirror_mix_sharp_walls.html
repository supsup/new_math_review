Back to the wall at five halves

The last wall is made of prime angles

The farthest a zero can go depends on how much the primes can turn the ratio of the two L-functions. The 45° mix needs a turn of about 148°. The 135° mix needs only about 32°, so its zeros can travel much farther right.

The sharp edge is near 1.120362498 at 45° and 2.382286109 at 135°. Zeros can get arbitrarily close from the left at each setting. The larger value is also a wall for the whole dial. At the wall itself the primes cannot all line up exactly.

These edges allow any height. In the band up to height 201, the 135° mix’s rightmost zero has real part about 2.34551234. See its certified disk and the other 26 pairs.

Where the prime angles come from

Divide the Euler product for L(s, χ) by that for its conjugate character. The factors cancel at primes where χ is real. When χ is i or −i, the remaining factor has the form (1 + iz)/(1 − iz) for z = ±p−s.

Multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator makes the numerator 1 − |z|² + 2i Re(z). Its largest turn in either direction is 2 arctan(p−σ), where σ is the real part. Add those limits over primes with residue 2 or 3 modulo 5 to get the curve A(σ).

The root number is ε = exp(iθ). At 135° the ratio must equal ε, which needs θ. At 45° it must equal −ε, which needs a turn of π − θ in the other direction. For any real mix, the ratio must lie on the line through ε, so θ is the smallest turn that can work. At the limiting real part, the factor for 2 would need its phase exactly opposite to the factor for 3 in the inputs 2−it and 3−it. One must be +1 and the other −1. This is impossible because log 2 and log 3 have irrational ratio.

Why zeros can approach the wall

Any finite set of distinct primes has rationally independent logarithms, by unique factorization. Kronecker–Weyl approximation lets the height t bring their phases as close as desired to the signs that make every turn point in the chosen direction. Absolute convergence gives the same approximation locally uniformly on Re s > 1 when we take more primes and move the heights out.

For real σ, the limiting quotient is exp(iA(σ)) for the positive turn, and exp(−iA(σ)) for the negative turn. It reaches exactly the required unit value at the corresponding wall. The sum A decreases strictly there, with negative derivative, so the limiting difference from the required value has a simple zero. Rouché’s theorem gives zeros of the actual translated functions in arbitrarily small disks around it.

This explains the edges in Bombieri and Ghosh’s analysis of the two Davenport–Heilbronn-type functions. Righetti also reports the 135° value. Bombieri and Ghosh, Theorem 7 and Section 6 · Righetti, page 2138 · Weyl on equidistribution.

How the numbers were enclosed

The direct prime sum converges slowly near 1. For the relevant primes, the quotient of the principal mod-5 series by the quadratic mod-5 series gives the factor (1 + r)/(1 − r), with r = p−σ. Its logarithm sums 2 artanh(r). To get the desired sum of 2 arctan(r), subtract their difference, which starts with 4r³/3. The omitted terms of this faster sum are bounded by an integral.
