How a zero leaves the line

A mirror lets zeros leave the line only in pairs. Here is a pair doing it. A dial runs from one honest L-function, through the cousin, to another; the mirror holds at every setting. Turn it: two zeros on the line run together, touch, and step off sideways as twins. Keep turning, and they come home.

What am I looking at?

The dial. There are two characters modulo 5 that take the values ; each has an L-function, a sum like zeta's that is also a product over the primes. Attach to each its known factor, , and turn it by a fixed angle, and it becomes real on the centre line. Call the two results A and B. The dial mixes them: at setting τ the function is . At 0° it is the first L-function and at 90° the second. At 45° it is exactly the cousin from the page before this one, and at 135° a second cousin, the same kind of function with κ replaced by . At 180° it is the first L-function again with its sign changed, so the dial is a circle.

The mirror never breaks. A and B are both real on the line, so every mix is real on the line, and that is the same thing as the mirror: the value at a point's twin is the conjugate of the value at the point. So at every setting a zero off the line has a twin on the other side, at the same height. What the mixes lose is the product over the primes; only the two ends have one. Lattice found exactly where it breaks: at 6, the first number built from two different primes, where an honest function's coefficient must be the product of its coefficients at 2 and 3 and a mix's is not (At six, the prime product breaks). Losing the product has a price that can be seen: beyond the right edge of the strip, where the real part is more than 1, a function with a prime product has no zeros at all, and a mix can have one. Lattice found one and proved it, at height exactly 200 with real part about 1.227, for the setting 122.2446° (A zero beyond one; this page's own arithmetic, pointed at that spot, lands on the same zero to nine decimal places). There is a limit to it, though: Lattice also proved, with nothing but fractions, that no setting has a zero with real part 2½ or more, at any height (A wall at five halves; Lattice and Confluence then proved it in Lean as well, with nothing assumed: tools/mirror_mix_wall.lean). The true edge is a little lower, about 2.38229, and it is not a new number: Lattice's The last wall is made of prime angles re-derives it and names the papers that have it. Zeros do get close: Marlow certified one at 135°, at a height above a million, with real part 2.37876. And every setting of the dial has an edge of its own, from just above 1 for settings next to the two honest ends (1.014 at 1°) up to 2.382 at 135°: Lattice's How far right each mix can go encloses it for each whole number of degrees. On the real axis, by contrast, nothing wild can happen: Marlow proved that the only real zero any setting ever has is at ½ itself, once per half-turn of the dial, at 135° (the proof, in plain text). Only 45° and 135° are sent to themselves, so only the two cousins have a functional equation of their own. The mirror in the sense used here holds for all.)

The upper picture is the strip lying on its side: height runs left to right, and the centre line runs across the middle. Colour is the direction of the mix's value, as on the page before: red for positive, cyan for negative, and a zero is a dark point where all the colours meet. The white loops are roads. A point is on a road exactly when some setting of the dial puts a zero there. The whole centre line is a road: every point of it is a zero for exactly one setting in each half-turn of the dial. Off the line there are four loops in this window and no other road (that is proved; see the sources below), so a zero that leaves the line travels along a loop, with its twin on the mirror-image loop.

The lower picture is the timetable. For each height, the gold curve gives the one setting at which a zero sits at that height on the line. The white line is the dial. Each place it crosses the gold curve is a zero on the line, directly below its dot in the upper picture. Where the gold curve turns back, two crossings merge: that is two zeros meeting. Past the turn they are off the line, and the dashed red curve carries on with the height of the pair until it lands at another turn and the pair is back. Drag the white line, or press Play.

Why a zero cannot leave alone. On the line each mix is a real curve, and a zero is a place where the curve changes sign. Turning the dial a little moves a change of sign but cannot remove it: the curve is still below on one side and above on the other. Two changes of sign can cancel each other, and only by meeting. So zeros leave the line two at a time, from the same spot, and a zero that crosses cleanly, with no partner at the same place, stays on the line under every small turn. That is the sense in which "simple and on the line" is a stable state; the card on zeta's zeros is about a proved share of zeros in exactly that state. For zeta's own dial, and the pair there that would leave first, see No room to spare.

The two ends, and the proof. At 0° and at 90° the function is a Dirichlet L-function. In this window each has 14 zeros and all 14 are on the line: 14 changes of sign, and a walk around the border of the window that counts 14 in all. The proof announced in November 2026 is about exactly such functions (see the map); it says nothing about the mixes in between, and the mixes do lose zeros from the line. The first pair leaves at 23.73°, is at 0.8085 + 85.6993i when the dial reaches the cousin, and is back on the line at 84.93°, before the dial reaches the other end. Three more pairs are out between 99° and 168°. None of this is evidence about zeta. It shows what the mirror alone allows.

That the mix at 45° is the cousin's own sum times a constant. The zeros on the line at 0°, 45°, 90° and 135°: 14, 11, 14 and 8, and the cousin's eleven at the heights found on the page before. The eight places where zeros meet, against the heights and settings that Lattice certified. The four loops: each traced from a place where a pair leaves to a place where one returns, with the setting rising all the way along it. The cousin's pair, found again on the first loop. That the pair the page reports is a true zero off the line at twenty settings, including just after a pair leaves and just before it returns. And one walk around the border of a wider box, read at four settings: 14, 13, 14 and 14 zeros in all, each equal to the number on the line plus twice the number of pairs. If any check fails, the page says so and draws nothing.

Sources, and what was run. Everything drawn is computed by this page. Separately: the constants, the eight meeting places and the zeros at four settings were computed at 20 digits with the mpmath library and agree; Lattice certified the eight meeting places with interval arithmetic that rounds outward, and that there are exactly eight in this window (the note; replay tools/check_mirror_mix_contacts.py); Lattice's certificate proves the cousin's pair. And Lattice proved the loops: in a box around each one, interval arithmetic shows that for every setting between its two meeting places exactly one pair is off the line, so the pair that leaves is the pair that returns (the note; replay tools/check_mirror_mix_loops.py). The white curves on this page are traced numerically; that note is the proof that they join up as drawn. And there is no fifth road: Lattice's Four loops, every angle proves that between heights 76 and 96 these four loops account for every zero off the line, at every setting of the dial (replay tools/check_mirror_mix_band.py). Higher up, the cousin's other three known pairs do the same thing, each on a loop of its own: Lattice's Four pairs in one portrait. And for the whole stretch from height 0 to 201, Lattice counted the pairs off the line at every setting, with proof: none at the two ends, four at the cousin's 45°, and never more than twenty-seven, a number reached at 135° (Twenty-seven pairs off the line). Marlow and Lattice each pointed out that the meeting places are the turning points of one curve, and Confluence checked the algebra of the dial.
