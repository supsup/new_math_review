A quartic recovers five missing readings

Put 58 inputs on a circle and number them 0 through 57. Reading i adds the
inputs at offsets 0, 4, 14, 36, 43, 46 and 48 from i, wrapping around at 58.
The readout keeps the remainder modulo 59. Any five missing readings can
be replaced by direct observations of any five distinct inputs.

The ordinary determinant is 3,047,747,308,987,465, which is
4,263,035 times 59 to the fifth power. Modulo 59 the rank is exactly 53.
This is a seven-connection construction for each reading. A lower bound
of six follows from the requirement that any five input coordinates vary
independently on the kernel. An equation with one through five nonzero
coefficients would violate that independence. Ordinary invertibility
excludes any empty row.

Why five direct inputs work

Take the polynomial with a term for each of the seven offsets. Its roots
among the 58 nonzero residues modulo 59 are exactly
23, 46, 33, 7 and 14. They form the progression 23 times 2^k for k from
0 through 4. The evaluation at a root shows that its geometric sequence
is invisible to the mixer. These five independent sequences span the
kernel. Therefore any change invisible to the readings has the form

    change at j = 23^j P(2^j)

where P is a polynomial of degree at most four. The addresses 2^j are
all distinct modulo 59. Five direct observations, after subtracting any
one consistent base solution and dividing by 23^j, determine P at five
distinct points. Two such polynomials agreeing at five points must coincide.

The transpose is the original mixer with both circular orders reversed.
A dependency among the rows left after five readings are lost would give
a nonzero vector in the left kernel with zeros in those five positions.
The same polynomial argument rules that out. Thus the surviving readings
keep exactly the original five dimensions of ambiguity, and the direct
observations resolve them.

Replay with python-flint installed

    python3 -B tools/check_five_loss_mixer.py

The replay checks the integer determinant, the finite-field rank and roots,
and actual row replacement on 128 fixed erasure/probe pairs. All
4,582,116 probe sets are covered by the distinct-address argument.

A larger board meets the six-connection lower bound

At prime 14717, use 14716 inputs and just the offsets

    0, 1912, 4076, 5184, 12912, 13005.

Among the 14716th roots modulo 14717, the wiring polynomial vanishes
exactly at 16, 32, 64, 128 and 256. The ratio is again 2, now of order
14716. The same quartic argument therefore gives recovery from any five
lost readings and any five direct inputs. The ordinary determinant is
nonzero by the integer-polynomial gcd with T^14716 − 1.

The six taps came from a generating function. Work modulo 14717 and
take the terms through degree six in exp(t + 13758 t² + 6281 t³). Call
this polynomial E(t). The next two coefficients vanish, so through
degree eight log E(t) equals t + 13758 t² + 6281 t³. Here
E(t) = ∏(1 + x_i t), with these six distinct tap addresses.

    3944, 5822, 12349, 4816, 13339, 3882.

The coefficient of t^k in each log(1 + x_i t) is
(−1)^(k−1) x_i^k / k. The vanishing log coefficients at degrees 4
through 8 give the five required power-sum zeros. Writing the addresses
as powers of 2 yields tap offsets. Subtracting the least offset from
all six amounts to a cyclic shift, which leaves the modular root set
unchanged. The replay checks that the wiring polynomial has exactly the
five specified roots.

Replay with python-flint installed

    python3 -B tools/check_six_tap_mixer.py

The replay uses polynomial gcds, so it never materializes the large
matrix. The same exact integer gcd and an independent gcd modulo
1,000,003 both certify ordinary invertibility. Six connections are
optimal for recovery at this size. The example supplies no minimum
claim for the prime or board size needed to attain six.
