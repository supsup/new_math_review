How big can a class number be?

For each imaginary quadratic field there is a whole number, its class number h, that measures how far its arithmetic is from having unique factorization. Dirichlet found that h is tied to a value of an L-function: for the field of discriminant −d (with d > 4), , where χ is the field's character. So the size of h is a question about the size of L(1, χ). If a generalized Riemann hypothesis holds, Littlewood showed in 1928 that L(1, χ) stays in a narrow band around 1, growing or shrinking no faster than log log d. The announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis ends with that hypothesis.

1 · Every field up to four million

Each teal bar spans the smallest and largest L(1, χ) among the fundamental discriminants in a narrow range of d; the gold dots are the record holders, each larger (or smaller) than every L(1, χ) before it. There are 1,215,854 fields here, every fundamental discriminant down to −4,000,000. The horizontal scale is log d, running on to 10¹² to show where the proven curves begin.

Read the curves with care. Littlewood's two curves carry an o(1): they say what happens as d grows without end, and at these sizes they are shapes, not fences. The smallest value found, 0.1988 at d = 991,027, is even below the conjectured eventual floor at that size, 0.257; it is above Littlewood's lower shape, 0.176. The largest, 6.350 at d = 2,155,919, is near the conjectured eventual ceiling, 6.53, and far below Littlewood's 9.55, which carries the factor of 2 still at issue. Only the gold curves, from 10¹⁰ on, are honest inequalities, and they are conditional.

3 · The same primes twice

The fields with the largest L(1, χ) are, again and again, the primes p that make you wait longest for a number that is not a square modulo p (the second wait on How long must you wait?). Of the 13 primes below four million that set a record for that wait, 11 also set a record here: 3, 7, 23, 71, 311, 479, 1559, 5711, 18191, 31391 and 366791. The reason is reciprocity. For a prime p ≡ 3 mod 4 and an odd prime q, the field's character at q is (−p/q) = (q/p): +1 exactly when q is a square modulo p. And 2 is a square modulo p exactly when p ≡ 7 mod 8, which is when the character at 2 is +1 too. So every prime before the first non-square gets the value +1, and those primes alone push L(1, χ) up by about times the logarithm of the wait. The two exceptions have honest reasons: 3,818,929 is 1 mod 4, so its field has discriminant −4·3,818,929, outside this range; and 10,559 waits long but is beaten by a smaller field. The record before it is already held by d = 10,391, with L(1, χ) = 4.7153 against 10,559's 4.6777. Through the prime 19 the Euler product of 10,559 is ahead, 5.847 to 5.262; but its character is −1 at all six primes from 23 to 43, and by 43 the order has flipped, 4.874 to 5.515. 4 · What is proved, and on what hypothesis

  1. Littlewood, 1928, as Granville and Soundararajan state it: on the generalized Riemann hypothesis, for the real primitive character of a fundamental discriminant d. Still on that hypothesis, both sides are attained to within a factor of 2 infinitely often; "only a factor of 2 remains at issue". The lower attainment was later proved without any hypothesis by Chowla.
  2. Explicit form: Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan (Theorem 1.5) prove, on the same hypothesis, for every primitive character χ to a modulus q ≥ 10¹⁰: and Their Corollary 1.3 turns this into bounds for h(−q). These are the gold curves.
  3. The archive. The hypothesis these use is that L(s, χ) has no zero with Re s > ½. The announced proof's last file states exactly that for every Dirichlet character (the statement DirichletLNonvanishingRightOfHalf, its theorem LFunction_ne_zero_of_half_lt_re). So if that proof stands, the gold curves are theorems for every field with d ≥ 10¹⁰. We have not checked that part of the archive; what we compiled is the last stretch, from two stated inputs.
  4. Our data is an exact count, not an estimate: every reduced form a·x² + b·xy + c·y² with b² − 4ac = −d, for every d up to 4,000,000, in one sweep; at a fundamental discriminant every form is primitive, so the count is h. Checked independently against Dirichlet's sum at 1,566 discriminants including both record holders, with no difference; and the discriminants with h = 1 come out as exactly the nine of Heegner, Baker and Stark.
