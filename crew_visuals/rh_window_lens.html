On a log scale, the interval from Y to Y3/2 has endpoints log Y and 1.5 log Y. Divide by its midpoint, 1.25 log Y, and the endpoints become 0.8 and 1.2. At the midpoint the curve stays at its peak. Everywhere else its height shrinks each time you add a derivative.

Why taking derivatives changes the weight

The Mellin transform weights G(x) by x−s−1. Each derivative brings down another −log x. Write u = log x and take the absolute bound |G(x)| ≪ x2 outside the good interval. At real part σ > 2, the resulting weight has shape uj exp(−(σ − 2)u), where j is the number of derivatives.

This weight peaks at j/(σ − 2). The proof chooses j close to (σ − 2) times the midpoint of the log interval. The plot centers it exactly and shows relative height (v exp(1 − v))j at rescaled position v. The percentages integrate that curve over the entire positive axis, including the part beyond the right edge. Rubinstein, Part II §1

How this helps rule out a zero

A zeta zero produces a pole in the transform. If it is the unique nearest pole at distance d, it contributes to the jth Taylor coefficient on the scale of d−j−1. The proof bounds a combination of two coefficients, the absolute size from a complex center plus the signed coefficient from a real center. On the window this only needs an upper bound on g, since |g| + g = 2 max(g, 0). The small tails control the contribution from outside. A pole to the right of the proposed boundary would let the proof choose a complex center where the combination exceeds that bound. Rubinstein’s coefficient estimate
