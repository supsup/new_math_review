One zero inside the circle

Zeta’s Davenport–Heilbronn cousin has zeros off the center line. Pick a reported location and a radius. A bound for the whole circle can certify that the disk contains exactly one zero.

The closer the center is to a zero, the smaller the first error. A smaller radius also reduces the size of the linear term. Try shrinking the circle until the inequality can no longer decide.

Why the two bars suffice

The cousin is 5−sH(s), where H(s) = ζ(s, 1/5) + κ[ζ(s, 2/5) − ζ(s, 3/5)] − ζ(s, 4/5) and κ ≈ 0.284079. The factor 5−s never vanishes, so we can work with H.

Around the center c, compare H(s) with the straight-line approximation H′(c)(s − c). The error is at most |H(c)| plus the radius times the largest change in H′ across the disk. The straight-line expression has one zero. If the error is strictly smaller than its magnitude around the entire rim, Rouché’s theorem gives the same count for H.

Where the locations came from

Davenport and Heilbronn published this example in 1936. Robert Spira reported the four pairs in 1994. A later note challenged the off-line zeros, and its authors subsequently corrected the claim after finding a wrong character in their calculation. These eight disks verify zeros at all four pairs. Does the rest of the window agree?

Between heights 76 and 96, with real part between −0.5 and 1.5, a separate interval check counts 13 zeros of the cousin and 9 of zeta. It subdivides the boundary until the image of each segment lies in a rectangle that excludes zero. Each turn of the image around zero therefore contributes to a certified count. The completed cousin changes sign in 11 disjoint intervals on the center line, and the two off-line disks at height 85.7 give the other two zeros.
