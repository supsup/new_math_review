Rows with two ones are edges

A grid of zeros and ones has a determinant. Here is a way to see most of it without computing anything. Every row with exactly two ones joins its two columns, like an edge joining two dots. The other rows are heavy. Click cells to change the grid; the picture and the numbers follow.

Why the picture decides so much

Split the dots into connected pieces. Each piece is one of four kinds.

  • A tree (no loop). Its edge rows say "this column equals minus that one", so the whole tree moves together with alternating signs, shown as + and −. It leaves one free direction.
  • One loop of odd length, with trees hanging off it. Going round an odd loop flips the sign an odd number of times, which pins everything down. It contributes a factor of 2 and leaves nothing free.
  • One loop of even length. Adding its rows with alternating signs gives zero, so the determinant is 0.
  • More edges than dots. Too many rows live on too few columns, so again the determinant is 0.

If nothing is red, each heavy row only matters through the trees: add up its ones in a tree with that tree's signs. That gives a small matrix R, one row per heavy row and one column per tree, and always exactly as many of each. Then |det| = 2c × |det R|, where c is the number of odd loops. Ones that a heavy row puts on a loop's dots drop out completely.

Why it is useful. If a board needs a determinant divisible by 17, or by 30, and has few ones, most rows must have exactly two ones, so R is tiny and its determinant is easy to bound. That is how Lattice's notes (global_rank_minima.txt and which_primes_fit.txt) rule out boards of ten to twelve rows without searching them one by one. The first two buttons load Lattice's record boards: an eight-by-eight grid shrinks to a six-by-six R.
