How far apart can two primes be?

After a prime p, how long until the next one? Usually about log p steps, but sometimes much longer. If the Riemann Hypothesis holds, the wait is never longer than (22/25)·√p·log p, for every prime p after 3. That is the 2019 theorem of Carneiro, Milinovich and Soundararajan, sharpening Cramér's of 1920. The announced proof ends with the Riemann Hypothesis itself, so if it stands this bound is simply true. The record gaps actually found are far smaller still.

1 · Every record gap up to ten billion

A gold dot is a gap longer than every gap before it, 35 of them up to 10¹⁰, the last 354 after 4,302,407,359. Both scales are logarithmic.

The same records, as a fraction of the RH bound. The closest any prime after 3 comes, among all primes below 10¹⁰ and not only the records, is the gap from 7 to 11, which uses 88.3% of the bound. Later records use much less, though not steadily less: the gap of 14 after 113 uses 31.7%, more than the 21.5% of the gap of 8 after 89 before it. By the last record the share is 0.028%. (In the normalization the theorem is stated in, gap/(√p·log p), that closest case is 0.777 against the constant 22/25 = 0.88.) After 3 the gap of 2 would use 119% of the bound, which is why the theorem starts after 3.

3 · What RH buys, and what it does not

  1. Cramér, 1920: on the Riemann Hypothesis the gap after p is O(√p·log p), with an unspecified constant.
  2. Carneiro, Milinovich and Soundararajan, 2019 (Theorem 5): "Assume the Riemann hypothesis. Then, for x ≥ 4, there is always a prime number in the interval [x, x + (22/25)√x log x]." In gap form, pn+1 − pn ≤ (22/25)√pn log pn for every prime pn > 3. Their proof covers x up to 4·10¹⁸ by the known computation of prime gaps (gaps below (log p)² there, by Oliveira e Silva, Herzog and Pardi), and the rest by the explicit formula. Asymptotically their constant is below 21/25. They improve Dudek, Grenié and Molteni's 1 + 4/log x.
  3. The archive. Its first final theorem is the Riemann Hypothesis itself, in the form of Lean's mathematical library (riemannHypothesis : RiemannHypothesis in Main.lean). So if the proof stands, the bound in item 2 holds for every prime after 3. We have not checked that part of the archive; what we compiled is the last stretch, from two stated inputs.
  4. Not Legendre. Legendre conjectured a prime between any two consecutive squares, an interval of length about 2√x. The RH interval, 0.88·√x·log x, is shorter only up to about x = 13; from x = 14 on it is longer, by a growing factor of log x. So this RH gap bound leaves Legendre's conjecture open. (Whether the Riemann Hypothesis implies Legendre's conjecture by some other argument is not known.)
  5. Not Cramér's guess. The records hug (log p)²: the last one is 0.72·(log p)². Cramér conjectured gaps of that size, and it is far beyond what any hypothesis of this kind is known to give.
Sources, and how this was computed

Read: E. Carneiro, M. B. Milinovich and K. Soundararajan, "Fourier optimization and prime gaps", arXiv 1708.04122 (Comment. Math. Helv. 94 (2019)), Theorem 5, Corollary 4 and the introduction. The record gaps were compared with the OEIS entries A002386 (the primes) and A005250 (the gaps): all 35 up to 10¹⁰ agree, and the next record, 382 after 10,726,904,659, lies just past our range.

Our own computation is a segmented sieve of Eratosthenes to 10¹⁰ (tools/prime_gap_records.c, 22 seconds); it counts 455,052,511 primes, the standard value of π(10¹⁰). tools/check_prime_gaps_rh.py reruns it and compares with this page and with the OEIS table. The finder above uses a deterministic Miller–Rabin test (the first twelve primes as bases, exact below 3·10²³), checked on load against trial division.
