The prime-sum window

Why fourth powers are enough

The prime-window criterion asks for a bound at every real point in a long interval. If you can bound the smoothed prime sum at the fourth powers around that interval, convexity fills in the gaps at a cost of order √x.

What happens between samples

Each prime power n contributes a nonnegative multiple of max(x/n − 1, 0). Its contribution starts at zero and then rises along a straight line. Add those contributions and the linear correction to see that G(x) + x log x is convex. A convex curve lies below the chord joining any two of its points.

Suppose the two samples a and b satisfy G(a) ≤ aη and G(b) ≤ bη, with 0 ≤ η ≤ 1. The chord for xη lies below the curve. This leaves only the difference between the chord for x log x and its curve. Its second derivative is 1/x, at most 1/a throughout the interval. Thus

G(x) ≤ xη + (b − a)²/(8a).

Derive the factor of 1/8

The function x²/(2a) − x log x is convex because its second derivative is 1/a − 1/x ≥ 0. That bounds the chord error of x log x by the chord error of the quadratic. At x = (1 − t)a + tb, this is (b − a)²t(1 − t)/(2a). Since t(1 − t) ≤ 1/4, the displayed bound follows.

Why the exponent is four

For a = m⁴ and b = (m + 1)⁴, the gap is at most 15m³ for m ≥ 1. Squaring the gap and dividing by a leaves a multiple of m², which is √a. In fact the bound above is at most 29√x.

Switch to fifth powers and move the slider right. The midpoint ceiling grows in units of √x. For a grid of integer r-th powers, its scale is x1 − 2/r. The exponent is 1/2 at r = 4 and 3/5 at r = 5.

Why convexity alone cannot improve that scale

Join the sample values of x log x by line segments, then subtract x log x. The resulting example is zero at every sample and reproduces the green curve between samples. Its log-corrected function is convex because the chord slopes increase. At a midpoint the error is between (b − a)²/(8b) and (b − a)²/(8a), by the lower and upper bounds on the second derivative. This example uses only the convexity information. The actual prime sum has additional arithmetic structure.

The fourth-power criterion

For each η strictly between 1/2 and 1, find arbitrarily large positive integers N for which G(m⁴) ≤ (m⁴)η holds at the samples m = N through ⌊N3/2⌋ + 1. The final sample reaches past the end of [N⁴, N⁶].

To get the continuous criterion at an exponent θ > 1/2, choose η strictly between 1/2 and θ. For large x, xη + 29√x ≤ xθ. The retained Lean prime-window theorem then gives the Riemann hypothesis from these cofinal sample bounds. The classical estimate in the other direction gives the sample bounds under RH. What if the prime weights have signs?

The archive also uses a weighted sum of corrected prime sums for a fixed family of Dirichlet characters. Some hinges can point down. A hinge at n acts like a line on each side of n, so only hinges with a < n < b affect the error between samples.

For a single unit-weight hinge, at x = (1 − t)a + tb, the chord gap is min(t(b − n), (1 − t)(n − a))/n. It is at most (b − a)/(4a). The absolute weight here is at most log b times the sum of the fixed family weights. There are at most b − a integer locations between consecutive fourth powers. This gives a total hinge error of order √x log x. The coefficient of the x log x correction is the sum of the fixed weights on the trivial characters. That correction costs order √x, and linear corrections have zero chord error.

The same exponent margin absorbs √x log x. On paper this extends the sufficient fourth-power criterion to a fixed archive family.
