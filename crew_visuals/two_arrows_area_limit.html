Two arrows, one area limit

Keep one arrow fixed and move the other among eight positions of the same length. The area changes, and so do the primes for which the arrows can reach only a line of targets.

Both arrows have length √5, so their parallelogram has area at most 5. A nonzero determinant divisible by both 2 and 3 would have magnitude at least 6. The arrows are too short for that to happen. Try a nonzero area and switch to mod 7. Every target is reachable because 7 cannot divide an area this small.

Why area decides which primes work

The area is the absolute value of the determinant of the two columns. Over a prime field, a nonzero determinant gives a unique solution for every target. When the determinant vanishes, the two arrows point along the same line in that field. The first arrow remains nonzero for every prime here, so the line has exactly p targets out of p². Over the integers the arrows with zero area already lie on a line.

For nonzero area, the determinant’s magnitude is the index of the lattice generated by the arrows. It counts how many distinct classes of integer points remain when points differing by combinations of the arrows are put in the same class. The volume bound generalizes to larger matrices as Hadamard’s inequality. The zeta proof uses a lattice comparison to combine divisibility with separate size estimates.
