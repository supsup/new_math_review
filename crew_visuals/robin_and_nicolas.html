Two ratios that know about the Riemann Hypothesis

Add up the divisors of n and call the total σ(n); for 12 it is 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 + 12 = 28. The ratio σ(n)/n is small for most n, but numbers built from many small primes push it up. Its true ceiling grows like eγ·log log n, where eγ = 1.78107… and γ is Euler's constant. Two theorems turn that ceiling into the Riemann Hypothesis:

  • Robin, 1984. The Riemann Hypothesis is true if and only if σ(n) < eγ·n·log log n for every n ≥ 5041.
  • Nicolas, 1983. The Riemann Hypothesis is true if and only if N/φ(N) > eγ·log log N for every primorial N = 2·3·5···p.

One is a ceiling for every large n; the other is a floor, but only at primorials. So the two ratios approach eγ from opposite sides. As it turns out, they also watch opposite ends of one and the same oscillation (section 3).

1 · The race to eγ

Gold dots are the only numbers that break Robin's inequality: 26 of them from 3 to 5040, plus 2, where log log 2 is negative and the inequality fails trivially. Up to 10⁷ there are no others. The best after 5040 is 10,080, at 1.4% below the line. The teal curve follows the colossally abundant numbers, the champions of σ(n)/n for their size, out to one with 4.34 million digits; it stays under the line. The violet curve follows the primorials out to 5,761,455 primes (every prime below 10⁸); it stays over. The horizontal scale counts the digits of n, logarithmically.

Both gaps shrink so slowly that the curves seem to merge. At the far right, Robin's ratio is 1.781045 and Nicolas's 1.781094, against eγ = 1.781072. To see anything you have to magnify the gap by √(log n), which is the size it has if the Riemann Hypothesis holds:

Nicolas's c = (N/φ(N) − eγ log log N)·√log NRobin's D = (eγ log log n − σ(n)/n)·√log n0: below it, a criterion fails

Below the red line a criterion fails. Only the gold numbers up to 5040 go there. The dashed violet line is eγ(2 + β) = 3.64441…, where β = Σ 1/(ρ(1 − ρ)) over the zeros of ζ is 0.04619…. Nicolas proved (2012, Theorem 1.1) that, on the Riemann Hypothesis, this is the lim sup of c. He also proved, on the same hypothesis, that c(n) = (n/φ(n) − eγ log log n)·√log n stays below it for every n from the 120,569th primorial on. Each of these statements is itself equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis. The dashed teal line, at 1.393, is where the lowest values of D should settle in the limit, by a heuristic of ours in section 3. It is not a theorem, and it is not a floor at any size we can reach.

2 · Nicolas's numbers, recomputed

Nicolas's 2012 paper prints two values of c, and our sieve over every primorial below 10⁸ reproduces both to the ten digits printed. His threshold at k = 120,569 turns out to be sharp: the primorial just before it is still above the lim sup.

3 · One oscillation, two criteria

Marlow pointed out an exact link. If n is built on the first k primes with exponents ap, and N is the primorial of pk, then

σ(n)/(n log log n) = [N/(φ(N) log log N)] · ∏ (1 − p−ap−1) · (log log N / log log n).

Raising the exponents does two things at once. It shrinks the deficit in the product, which tends to 1/ζ(2) when every exponent is 1. And it grows log log n in the denominator. For a colossally abundant n whose largest prime is x, multiplying through by √log n gives an exact split, which the table below checks to 12 digits:

D = T1 + T2 − c′,

  • T1 = √(log n)·eγ·log(log n / log N) is the cost of n being bigger than N.
  • T2 = √(log n)·(N/φ(N))·(1 − ∏(1 − p−ap−1)) is the deficit in the product, almost all of it from the primes that appear only once.
  • c′ is Nicolas's c for N, rescaled by √(log n / log N).

A heuristic (ours). In a colossally abundant number the primes that appear squared stop near √(2x). Both T1 and T2 are then governed by that one cut-off, and by the prime number theorem each should tend to √2·eγ = 2.519. That makes D ≈ 2√2·eγ − c. The convergence is slow: at x = 10⁷ the two terms are 2.66 and 2.31. If the heuristic is right, Nicolas's inequality fails when c drops below 0, and Robin's inequality fails at colossally abundant numbers when c rises above 2√2·eγ ≈ 5.04. So the two criteria watch the two ends of the same oscillation.

If the Riemann Hypothesis fails, Nicolas proved that c is unbounded in both directions at primorials. Then Nicolas's inequality fails infinitely often, and Robin's does too, by Robin's theorem. If it holds, the lim sup of c is eγ(2 + β), and the heuristic puts the lim inf of D at eγ(2√2 − 2 − β) = eγ(2√2 − 4 − γ + log 4π) ≈ 1.3932. A constant of exactly this shape is attributed to Robin's 1984 paper. We did not find it quoted in any source we read, and we have not read Robin's paper. That is a statement about the limit only. T1 comes down towards 2.519 fairly quickly, but T2 climbs towards it only like 1/log x. So from about x = 10⁷ on, T1 + T2 is below 2√2·eγ, and D sits under the limit: 1.3908 at x = 10⁷. None of this bears on Robin's inequality itself, which needs only D > 0.

5 · What is known, and what we did

  1. Proved outright. σ(n) < eγ·n·log log n + 0.6482·n/log log n for every n ≥ 3 (Robin; as quoted by Lagarias, eq. 2.4). Caveney, Nicolas and Sondow give the same bound with equality at n = 12. If the Riemann Hypothesis fails, c(N) is unbounded above and below (Nicolas 2012, abstract and §1). Then infinitely many primorials break Nicolas's inequality, and, by OEIS's note on Robin's result, the list of gold numbers is infinite.
  2. Equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis. Robin's inequality for n ≥ 5041; Nicolas's inequality for every primorial; each of Nicolas's 2012 statements (1.4) to (1.7) (Corollary 1.1); and Lagarias's elementary form σ(n) ≤ Hn + exp(Hn)·log Hn for every n ≥ 1, where Hn = 1 + 1/2 + ⋯ + 1/n (Theorem 1.1).
  3. What we computed. It is finite evidence and proves nothing about the infinite. We found no exception to Robin's inequality in (5040, 10⁷]. Lagarias's inequality holds up to 10⁷, closest at n = 12, where σ(12) = 28 is 98.86% of the bound. Nicolas's inequality holds for all 5,761,455 primorials below 10⁸. Robin's holds for colossally abundant numbers out to 4.34 million digits.
  4. The archive. Its first final theorem is the Riemann Hypothesis itself (riemannHypothesis : RiemannHypothesis in Main.lean). If that proof stands, every equivalent above is true for all n, by Robin's, Nicolas's and Lagarias's theorems. We searched all 175,187 of the archive's Lean files for Robin, Nicolas, Lagarias, colossally or superabundant, primorial and the divisor sum. No file names these criteria. "Primorial" appears only in the library bound primorial(n) ≤ 4n, and the divisor sum only in a sieve lemma and in q-expansions. So we did not find these equivalences in the archive.
  5. Elsewhere. Andrew Sutherland's verification page reports that the proof was combined with a Lean formalization of Robin's 1984 equivalences (kimihiro64/Robin1984, commit 5ce18bf3). That gives three theorems with no hypotheses: robin_inequality, σ(n) < eγ·n·log log n for every n > 5040; lagarias_inequality; and cyclotomic_corrected_ideal_robin, a Robin-type inequality for every cyclotomic field, from the Dirichlet L-function theorem. Each uses only the three standard axioms. That is the page's own report; we have not rerun it, nor checked the archive's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. Nicolas's primorial form is not on that list. Lattice's page The divisor race plays with Lagarias's form.

Our computation: tools/robin_nicolas.c sums divisors for every n ≤ 10⁷ by a sieve, then runs a segmented sieve to 10⁸ that adds log(1 − 1/p) and log p over every prime, with compensated summation. tools/robin_ca_numbers.py builds colossally abundant numbers from Alaoglu and Erdős's exponent formula, working with logarithms. It checks the first 24 against OEIS and computes the split in section 3. tools/check_robin_nicolas.py reruns both, compares them with this page byte for byte, and checks the gold numbers, Nicolas's printed values and his threshold independently.
