Once the surviving mixed readings are fixed, two numbers remain to be determined. Each direct input gives one equation in those two numbers. Distinct slopes mean the two equations have a unique solution. Try choosing the same input twice to see why repetition fails.

Why throwing away any two reports works

With the full board, the space of inputs that read as zero is two-dimensional. Each pair of input coordinates gives an invertible map from this space to a pair of values. Turning the board over swaps inputs and reports. For these rotating boards that is just reversal of the circular numbering, so every pair of report coordinates is independent in the space of dependencies among reports too.

A dependency among the surviving reports would be a dependency with zeros at the two removed positions. That would give a nonzero vector in the report dependency space with two zero coordinates, which its independent pairs rule out. The remaining reports therefore have full row rank. Their kernel still has dimension two and equals the full board’s kernel. The two direct inputs resolve exactly that ambiguity.

How the rotations build the slopes

Write the wiring offsets as exponents of a polynomial f. For the mod 23 board, row zero adds inputs 0, 1 and 4, so f(T) = 1 + T + T4. If λ is a common root of f and Tn − 1, then the sequence 1, λ, λ², … reads as zero in every row. Each example has exactly two such roots α and β, and they account for the two-dimensional kernel. The jth direct input has coefficients (αj, βj). Divide both by αj to get its slope (β/α)j.

The ratio β/α has order n in each field, so the slopes traverse all n distinct nonzero residues. Two different slopes give a nonzero two-by-two determinant. The mod 19 example is Marlow’s. The three examples and the search below are checked with the exact replay.

How small can a rotating board be?

There are only p + 1 directions in a two-dimensional space modulo p, so a board can have at most that many inputs. The exact replay checks every binary rotating pattern, up to rotation, at sizes 3 through p + 1 for each prime through 17. The first modulus that works is 17, at size 16 with five marks per row as the minimum. Marlow’s size-10 mod 11 example shows that other wiring patterns can work at a smaller modulus.

Three marks per row at mod 23 are the fewest possible for any ordinary-invertible rotating board that loses rank modulo an odd prime. With two marks, each report adds an input to its neighbor by a fixed step. On even cycles the alternating-sign input is already in the ordinary kernel. On odd cycles the alternating condition forces the input to equal its own negative, so the kernel is still zero modulo an odd prime.

For the search, the greatest common divisor g of f and Tn − 1 gives the recurrence for the kernel. Indeed, g is a combination of those two polynomials and divides both, so f and g kill the same vectors under the cyclic shift. Every degree-d recurrence solution has d free initial values and closes after n steps because g divides Tn − 1. The replay keeps only degree two over the finite field and degree zero over the rationals. It checks probe 0 against every offset using the recurrence. Rotation covers all other pairs, including the transpose.
