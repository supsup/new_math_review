Back to the 45° and 135° walls

How far right each mix can go

The two equal-weight mixes ask the primes for a turn. At an intermediate angle the primes also have to change the ratio’s size. The sharp wall is where the region they can cover just reaches both requirements.

At 30° and 120°, the required sizes are reciprocals. Because the regions are symmetric left to right, those sizes cost the same. The required turns differ, so the walls sit near 1.114034 and 2.017581 respectively.

At 120° I looked for a height where the first few prime phases nearly line up. That gave a zero near height 694,397,677.193 and real part 2.012362, about 0.00522 from its wall. Why one prime gives a convex region

Real parts greater than 1 let us divide the two Dirichlet Euler products. The factors that remain are (1 + z)/(1 − z) with |z| = r = p−σ, for primes p congruent to 2 or 3 modulo 5. Taking the logarithm turns their product into a sum.

Let x + iy be the logarithm of one factor. Filling in the disk |z| ≤ r gives the region

cosh x ≤ C cos y,   |y| < π/2,   C = (1 + r²)/(1 − r²).

To see it, invert the quotient and square its magnitude. The inequality is convex because cosh x − C cos y has positive second derivatives in both coordinates on this strip. The region is symmetric across both axes. For a unit outward normal (a,b), the boundary gradient is (sinh x, C sin y). Its squared length is C² − 1 on the boundary. That gives the point directly.

x = asinh(2ra/(1 − r²)),   y = asin(2rb/(1 + r²)).

Why the edge is sharp

Add one point from each prime region. These sums form a compact convex region by absolute convergence. As σ increases, the regions shrink continuously. In particular, the prime 2 region at a smaller σ contains the region at a larger σ plus a small disk. As σ tends to infinity the sum shrinks to zero. As σ decreases to 1, the sum contains disks of arbitrarily large radius. Each prime region contains a disk of radius r, and those radii have divergent sum in the limit. The principal-to-quadratic ratio in the two-angle calculation shows this. The principal series has a pole, the quadratic series has a convergent positive sum when grouped by periods, and the cubic correction stays bounded.

Cancellation requires the ratio L(s,χ)/L(s,χ̄) to equal −tan τ ε. Take the logarithm whose imaginary part is closest to zero. Choosing a longer turn only moves the point farther out, by the two symmetries and convexity. The critical σ puts that point on the boundary. Any larger σ puts it outside.

At a supporting line, choose each prime’s boundary point with the same outward normal. Their sum is the target point. Each point comes from the original circle |z| = r. Kronecker–Weyl approximation brings finitely many actual prime phases as close as desired, and absolute convergence controls the rest locally uniformly in Re s > 1. The limiting quotient hits the required value at the wall. It tends to 1 as its real part grows and the required value is different from 1, so that zero of the difference is isolated. Rouché’s theorem on arbitrarily small circles carries those zeros back to actual height translates.

The disk of radius r used in the growth step lies in the logarithm region by its support function. Taking z in the direction of the normal gives support at least 2r − 2r³/[3(1 − r²)] ≥ r for r ≤ ½. This follows by bounding the tail of the odd powers in log((1 + z)/(1 − z)).

What the interval replay checks

For an intermediate angle, the lower-endpoint check finds a boundary point with both absolute coordinates larger than the target’s. Reflections and convexity put a whole rectangle inside the region. At the upper endpoint a supporting line separates the target from the region. The omitted primes in each coordinate are bounded by 8 times the sum of p−3σ after subtracting the leading contribution from the principal-to-quadratic ratio. The equal-weight settings use the simpler one-coordinate test.
