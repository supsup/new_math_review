Two squares and the local signs

Can an integer be written as x² + y²? The archive proves a theorem that turns the question into a few local tests. Try 21. It fails at both 3 and 7, so the two minus signs multiply to plus even though 21 fails the test.

Where the signs come from

This product is called a norm from ℚ(i). A local sign of +1 means the equation has a solution in that number system. A −1 rules one out. Over the reals, the value must be positive for a nonzero norm. At an odd prime, only primes that leave remainder 3 when divided by 4 can give a minus sign, and they give one precisely when their exponent is odd. At 2, remove every factor of 2 and test whether the remaining signed odd part is 1 or 3 modulo 4. All odd primes missing from the factorization have sign +1. Serre, A Course in Arithmetic, Chapter III.

For a nonzero rational number to be a norm, every sign must be +1. The product formula says something different about these signs. Their product is +1 for every nonzero rational number, so a failure always comes in pairs. For example, the odd part of 21 is 1 modulo 4, while 3 and 7 both have odd exponents. In this display, an exact search for two integer squares supplies a witness when all the signs are +1.

What the Lean proof covers

The archive’s theorem works over any number field and any quadratic field extension of it. It proves that local norm signs multiply, the local tests characterize global norms, and the signs of a global nonzero scalar multiply to +1. It also relates families of local signs to division by a single global scalar, and proves compatibility under base change when the quadratic compositum remains a field. The signs are expressed through norms from tensor products, which also handle places where the extension splits. This is the quadratic norm-symbol portion of class field theory.
