How long must you wait?

The announced proof ends with five theorems, not one. Here is what the last three promise.

The proof that made the news is of the Riemann Hypothesis, a statement about one function, zeta. But the last file of the archive states five theorems. Two are about zeta. The other three say the same thing about a whole family of functions, the Dirichlet L-functions: one for each way of sorting whole numbers by their remainders. That larger statement has a name, the generalised Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet characters, and for a century people have proved things on the assumption that it is true. If the proof stands, each of those becomes a plain theorem. This page shows two of them. Both are promises about waiting.

A caution first. "If the proof stands" is doing real work in that sentence. We compiled about one tenth of the archive ourselves and found no fault in it (the record); others report building all of it. This page takes the five theorems as given and asks what follows.

The five endings, as the archive's last file Main.lean states them:
  1. riemannHypothesis: every zero of zeta, apart from the known ones at the negative even numbers, has real part one half.
  2. riemannZeta_eq_zero_re: the same sentence written out in full, without the library's name for it.
  3. LFunction_ne_zero_of_half_lt_re: no Dirichlet L-function has a zero with real part greater than one half. (It sets one point aside: for a trivial character, where the function has a pole and not a value.)
  4. LFunction_eq_zero_re: every zero of every Dirichlet L-function strictly inside the strip, real part between 0 and 1, has real part one half.
  5. LFunction_eq_zero_re_of_isPrimitive: the same for the basic members of the family, the primitive ones.

A detail we liked: the fourth says "strictly inside" for a reason. An L-function that is not one of the basic ones can have extra zeros, and when it does they sit exactly on the left edge of the strip, real part 0. The simplest case is the trivial character modulo 2, whose function is : the first factor vanishes at for every whole number k. So a correct statement has to leave that edge out, and this one does. (Not every such function has extra zeros; Marlow and Lattice supplied the exact scope.)

The first wait: a prime with a given remainder

Pick a number q to divide by and a remainder a that shares no factor with q. In 1837 Dirichlet proved that the list contains infinitely many primes. But how far along the list must you go to meet the first one? The five theorems put a cap on it.

Each spoke is one remainder. Its length is how far you count before the first prime with that remainder, on a scale where each step outward is ten times farther. The cap above is about 7.6 million, and the real longest wait is 13,921.

A surprise from the archive. Linnik's theorem is one of the published results the archive needs on its way, and it does not take it on trust: it proves it, in the form "some C and some L exist", and that proof uses only 517 of the archive's 175,187 files. We compiled it here. Lean accepts it and reports no assumption beyond its three standard axioms (tools/rh_linnik.lean prints the statement and the axioms; the run is in the record). So of the caps on this page, the weakest is the one we can vouch for outright: it needs no "if".

The second wait: a number that is not a square

Fix a prime p and look at remainders on division by p. Half of the nonzero remainders are squares, of the form , and half are not. Start counting at 2. How long until you meet one that is not a square? Many methods in computing begin by finding such a number, and nobody knows how to guarantee a quick find without a hypothesis. What changes from "if" to "then"

Three examples, each a result that was proved long ago with the words "assuming the generalised Riemann hypothesis" in front of it.

  • A fast test for primes becomes a proof. Miller's test of 1976 checks a number n against a few small "witnesses". On the same hypothesis Bach proved in 1990 that checking every witness up to settles the matter with certainty. (That is a theorem of its own, a relative of the second wait and not a consequence of it.) The page What RH buys runs it.
  • Square roots without dice. The standard way to take a square root modulo a prime starts by guessing numbers until one is not a square. The second wait says that counting 2, 3, 4, … finds one quickly, every time, so the method needs no luck.
  • Primes in every class, soon. The first wait, which is the first picture on this page.

What the three do not give

It would be easy to say that every old result "assuming the Riemann hypothesis" is now settled. That is not so, because there are many hypotheses of this kind and the archive proves one family of them. A well-known example is Artin's conjecture, that 2 is a "primitive root" for infinitely many primes (so that the decimal-style expansion of in base 2 is as long as it can be). Hooley proved it in 1967 on a hypothesis about a different family of zeta functions, those of certain number fields. For the base 2 that family includes the field you get by adjoining the cube roots of 2 and of 1, whose symmetries do not commute with one another, and no Dirichlet L-function describes a field like that. The five endings do not reach it.
