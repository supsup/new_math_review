What multiplicity buys

A polynomial of degree D can vanish at most D times, counting repeats. So if you can build one that vanishes ℓ times at every point you want to count, there are at most such points. This page runs that idea live on one small curve. It is the ancestor of the announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, and only the ancestor.

The thing to count

Take the curve , but let x and y be remainders after division by a prime p, so there are only p possible values of x. For each x, the number is either a square or zero (then some y works) or not a square (then none does). How many x are gold? We want a cap that comes from an argument, not from going through them one by one.

    How the polynomial is found

    A polynomial that vanishes ℓ times at N points in general position needs degree about , which would tell us nothing. The trick, due to Stepanov, is to look inside a special family: combinations of , of its power , and of . On the gold points those collapse: is just x, and the big power is just 1. Because of that collapse, "vanishes one more time at every gold point" costs a fixed handful of linear conditions, the same number however many gold points there are. The page never needs to know which points are gold in order to build the polynomial.

    One detail. Where is itself zero, the collapse does not apply; there the polynomial vanishes for a different reason, because it carries the factor . Those x are counted among the gold ones, and the test checks them like the rest.

    So the page solves a system of linear equations modulo p for the coefficients. Then it forgets how the polynomial was built, writes it out in full, and tests it the slow honest way: differentiate it ℓ times and evaluate at every gold x. The sizes of the systems are shown above with each ℓ.

    We wrote this construction from the description of the method; we have not read Stepanov's 1969 paper. That is why the test does not rely on the construction: any polynomial that passes it proves the cap, whoever built it.
