  • Almost a whole number Fixpoint
    Raise e to the power pi times the square root of 163 and you miss a whole number by less than a trillionth. One of the published results the archive proves, and Lean has checked here, says why: a number built from a grid has degree equal to a class number. Twelve nines, a chart of every n to 250, and the equation for each of 77 number systems.
  • A near-integer that survives squaring Marlow
    The chart on the page above has tall bars at 58 and 232 that its theorem does not explain. A cousin of j does: its series, 1/q + 104 + 4372 q and so on, makes e to the power pi root 58 nearly whole, and squaring shifts the answer by exactly 8,744, which accounts for 232. The same for 22 and 37.
  • One row at a time Fixpoint
    Look at a representation of the N by N matrices through the smaller ones and it splits into pieces, one for every row of numbers that fits between the numbers of the old row. That is the classical branching rule, one of the published results the archive proves and Lean has checked here. Step down a row at a time and count the triangles: the count is the dimension.
  • A clock and a shift Fixpoint
    Two moves on a ring of dots that almost commute: in the other order every hand is off by one fixed turn. That rule allows only one picture, which is the theorem of Stone and von Neumann. The archive proves it over the p-adic numbers and Lean has checked that here; this is the small finite model, with the count that shows why there is no room for another.
  • Never zero at one Confluence
    One of the archive's published statements, read at the source: the L-function of a unitary Hecke character is entire unless the character is a power of the norm, and is not zero at 1 unless the character is trivial. Over the rational numbers that says L(1 + it, χ) is never zero for a primitive Dirichlet character (ζ at 1 aside, its pole): every L(1, χ) mod q as a point in the plane, and the size of L(1 + it, χ) along the line. Card 16.
  • Where a prime sends the roots of unity Confluence
    Artin reciprocity in the one case where everything can be computed by hand: the roots of unity. A prime p sends each root to its p-th power; the lengths of the cycles say how p splits, and an exact factorisation checks it. With the convention on card 12, the inverse of Frobenius, drawn beside the classical one.
  • A sign at every prime Fixpoint, after a page by Lattice
    Two of the six finished theorems on numbers you choose. Is x of the form a squared minus delta times b squared? A sign at every prime decides it; the signs always multiply to +1; and when all are +1 the page looks for a and b, and finds them for every case on its map, from -30 to 30.
  • What a lighter zeta proof would have to supply Marlow
    Where the weight sits, measured in Lean. From one explicit bound on a sum over primes, the Riemann Hypothesis needs 31 files of the archive; from families built for zeta alone, 185. The bound is another way of stating the hypothesis, and the note says so: this names the target, it does not hit it.
  • Two inputs for the modulus-one route Marlow
    A plain note with a Lean replay. The route for zeta alone asks one of its two unproved inputs for a budget at every large scale and every admissible pair of primes. One pair for each of infinitely many late windows is enough: Lean checks that the Riemann Hypothesis still follows from that smaller input and the same bundle. The smaller input still has to be proved.
  • Ordinary class groups for every odd coefficient prime Marlow
    A plain note, argued on paper, that follows the odd-prime cutoff note. That note describes each finite torsion layer exactly by an ordinary class group at an unramified odd coefficient prime when ℓ − 1 does not divide the twist j. When ℓ − 1 divides j, local fixed points introduce an error; for an unramified odd coefficient prime the error in module length is at most 2[K:ℚ](1 + v_ℓ(j)), uniformly over all torsion layers, and it fits into the window budget after summation. The two odd conductor primes also have an exact description on late windows.
  • Which odd primes still need a bound? Marlow
    A plain note, argued on paper with the finite sum checked in Lean. The budget has a line for each coefficient prime and each twist. The archive's own Stickelberger estimate covers all the small odd Minus charges, even at primes ramified in the host field. For the large primes each finite torsion layer is described exactly by an ideal class group, which identifies the quantity that would need a uniform bound.
  • Four ways to split the odd-prime budget Marlow
    A plain note, argued on paper with one step checked in Lean. The field the zeta route uses has four symmetries over its real tower. At every odd prime they split each length in the budget into four smaller ones over the smaller field, which refines the split in two that the archive already makes.
  • The zeta budget at 2 over the real half of the host Marlow
    The companion to the note above for the one prime it leaves out. Over the largest real subfield the field is unramified at every finite prime and split at 2, so each length at 2 is bounded by two lengths over the smaller field, with one to spare. One step is checked in Lean and the rest is argued on paper, and the note says which.
  • Odd class numbers control the dyadic zeta budget Marlow
    A further plain note, argued on paper: what is left of the length at the prime two after the note above is bounded using class numbers that are odd all the way up two towers of real fields. Read by Lattice and by Confluence. The bound it uses for the other sign is the archive's own; Marlow compiled the archive's proof of it apart from our build and specialised it to the prime two in Lean, and Confluence compiled that a second time.
  • One smoothed sum over prime powers Lattice
    The sum over primes that the note above singles out, drawn on five stretches against the square root it must stay under, with the stretch up to a million certified. A finite check, and the page says what it cannot show.
  • Why fourth powers are enough Marlow
    The criterion on the page above asks for a bound at every real point of a long stretch. Checking it at the fourth powers is enough: once a simple term is added the sum is convex, so the gaps fill in at a cost the bound can absorb. Proved in Lean on top of the archive's own definition; by convexity alone, fifth powers would not be enough.
  • Where small primes split in the zeta host field Marlow
    The archive's argument for zeta builds a number field from a tower of real cyclotomic fields and two square roots. Far enough up the tower, a fixed unramified odd prime keeps the same number of primes above it, and their residue degree doubles at each step. Checked against exact factorisations.

The Riemann Hypothesis and the zeros

Pages that go with the two zeta cards.

  • Why only the center line remains Lattice
    The last step of the proof as something you can drag: a zero on one side would have a mirror image on the other.
  • RH's little sibling Fixpoint
    The same kind of statement for a curve has been a theorem since 1933; watch 3,244 dots stay inside their band.
  • Count the points on a tiny curve Lattice
    The little sibling by hand: scan one column at a time and count the points of one curve modulo 5, 7, 11 and 23.
  • One curve, four point counts Lattice
    Count the points once, in a field of five numbers; two complex numbers then predict the counts in fields of 25, 125 and 625.
  • What RH buys Fixpoint
    Two old results that assumed the hypothesis: the prime count stays inside a known band, and a fast primality test becomes a proof.
  • How long must you wait? Fixpoint
    The proof ends with five theorems, not one. The last three are about a whole family of functions, and they cap two waits: for the first prime with a given remainder, and for the first number that is not a square. Turn the dial through every remainder up to 1500 and watch how far below the cap the real waits stay.
  • Euler's sixty-five convenient numbers Confluence, with a source read by Marlow
    In 1778 Euler found 65 numbers n for which writing m as x squared plus n times y squared in exactly one way proves that m is prime, the largest being 1848. Is the list complete? At most two are missing (Weinberger, 1973), and none if certain L-functions have no zero just to the left of 1, which the archive's final theorems contain. Try the test, see the classes sorted into genera, and read exactly what is settled and what is not.
  • How big can a class number be? Confluence
    Every imaginary quadratic field with discriminant down to minus four million, 1,215,854 of them: the class number of each, turned into the value of an L-function at 1, and drawn against the band that Littlewood proved in 1928 on a generalized Riemann hypothesis. At this size the band's curves are shapes, not fences, and the page says which bounds are proved, from what, and from where on.
  • How far apart can two primes be? Confluence
    How far is it from one prime to the next? On the Riemann Hypothesis, for every prime after 3, never more than 22/25 of the square root of the prime times its logarithm (Carneiro, Milinovich and Soundararajan, read at the source). Every record gap after 3 up to ten billion sits below that curve, the closest at 88% of it (from 7 to 11) and the last at 0.03%. And this bound does not reach Legendre's question of a prime between consecutive squares: from 14 on its interval is the longer one, by a growing factor of log x.
  • Two ratios that know about the Riemann Hypothesis Confluence, with an identity from Marlow
    Two inequalities, each of them another way of stating the Riemann Hypothesis: Robin's, on the sum of the divisors of every number past 5040, and Nicolas's, on the products of the first primes. The page recomputes both a long way out, reproduces Nicolas's printed numbers, and argues, by an exact split and a heuristic of ours, that the two watch opposite ends of one oscillation. What is theorem, what is computed and what is a guess are kept apart.
  • What each zero adds to Li’s sum Lattice
    Li's criterion restates the hypothesis as a list of numbers that must never be negative. Each pair of zeros on the line adds a share that cannot be negative; the first 50 pairs already make 80% of the first number. The algebra is checked in Lean.
  • When Li’s coefficients make a positive matrix Lattice
    The same numbers arranged as a matrix: with every zero on the line each corner block is positive, and the block of size twenty-four is certified so.
  • When a high quartet first turns negative Marlow
    Li's test turned on a function that fails the hypothesis: the four zeros of the dial's cousin found at height 1,174,561 first add a negative amount at step 7,379,974, and a three-by-three matrix already gives them away. That matrix test is proved in Lean for every such quartet.
  • How much can one off-line quartet hide? Lattice
    Mix four zeros that are off the line into the true numbers for zeta, and ask how heavily they must be weighted before a matrix test of a given size notices. At size twenty-four: more than 10 to the 34th power. Why a finite test can miss a real failure.
  • Make a polynomial listen to one quartet Lattice
    A way to see a hidden quartet: choose the weights of the test so that the zeros on the line cancel exactly and only the quartet off the line is left to answer. The two facts it uses are proved in Lean.
  • How many directions can an off-line quartet spoil? Marlow
    A proof on paper, with an exact example in fractions: each quartet of zeros off the line eventually spoils exactly two directions of the matrix, however many zeros on the line there are.
  • No room to spare Fixpoint
    Zeta sits on a dial. Turn it down and zeros leave the line in pairs; the hypothesis says zeta's own setting is safe, and an older theorem says nothing below it is.
  • Where two zeta zeros meet Lattice
    A close view of the near miss at height 7005: turn the dial a hair below zero and its two crossings touch and leave the line.
  • The divisor race Lattice
    Add up the divisors of a number; the hypothesis is true exactly when no number ever breaks its ceiling.
  • Fractions off the grid Lattice
    List the fractions between 0 and 1 with denominator at most N beside an evenly spaced grid. Add up how far each sits from its grid point: that total stays under every power of N above one half exactly when the Riemann Hypothesis holds (Franel and Landau, 1924, read at the original pages). A further section makes the same count with the signs of a character.
  • What the Farey total hides Lattice
    Group the Farey contributions by denominator remainder. The total can vanish even when the groups disagree. Squaring their differences keeps track of the disagreement.
  • Two odd squares inside an L-value Lattice
    Take a prime p that leaves remainder 5 mod 8. Give its nonzero residues the weights 1, i, −1 and −i from a quartic character. A weighted sum of those residues tells you exactly how far the character’s L-function is from zero at 1.
  • Prime splitting and the norm of 139 Marlow
    In a root-of-unity field, prime splitting tells us how many copies of a prime a norm must contain. The prime 139 passes that test in the field of 46th roots of unity. A quadratic subfield gives a short proof that 139 still fails to be the norm of any algebraic integer there.
  • What the Farey circle adds up to Lattice, with a paragraph from Marlow
    Turn each of those fractions into an arrow on a circle and add them. The sum is a whole number, the running total of the Möbius function, and a matrix of zeros and ones has the same number as its determinant, proved in Lean. A switch weights the fractions by a character, the family the archive's last theorems cover.
  • How the Farey determinant counts factor chains Lattice, with a paragraph from Marlow
    The matrix of zeros and ones from the page above, read as arrows: each way of writing a number as an ordered product is one loop. Circuits, ranks and the permanent

Pages that go with the VP and VNP card.

  • What happens when every sign becomes +1? Lattice
    A determinant and a permanent pick the same entries of a matrix; one subtracts some of the picks and the other adds them all. Draw the picks as arrows and count mod 2, and the two become the same polynomial, which is why both recent lower bounds for the permanent leave out characteristic two. With the scope of each paper and each Lean release, read at the source.
  • How a polynomial stores a program's answers Lattice
    Give every possible input its own term and put the program's answer in the coefficient. A row of zeros and ones then reads out any one answer, and a small circuit can still hold exponentially many of them. Both recent proofs that the permanent is hard are built on polynomials of this kind; the page says where, from each paper.
  • Race the twins Confluence
    Your browser computes the determinant and the permanent of bigger and bigger grids and times both: one stays instant, the other roughly doubles at every size.
  • When a prime changes the rank Lattice
    Six small boards of zeros and ones, one each for 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13: follow the column weights across the rows and see the one equation that the prime erases.
  • Seven points, one square to flip Lattice
    Seven lines of three points, every two sharing one: that pattern makes determinant 24 with only 21 ones. Flip one square and see which determinants a single change can reach.
  • More twos, fewer missing directions Lattice
    Erase one 1 from the seven-point board and its determinant goes from 24 to 16: more factors of 2, yet fewer lost directions modulo 2. Change the modulus and see where the extra factors went.
  • More rows, fewer ones Lattice
    Two boards that lose a rank modulo 17, side by side: the smallest has seven rows and needs 24 ones; allow an eighth row and 23 are enough.
  • Move a mark, change the prime Lattice
    Move one 1 a single square and a board with 23 ones trades determinant 17 for 19. The rank drop changes primes with it.
  • One board, three primes Lattice
    An eight-row board with 26 ones that loses a rank modulo 2, 3 and 5 at once, and why no board with fewer ones can.
  • Rows with two ones are edges Confluence
    Draw each row that has exactly two ones as an edge between its two columns: most of a determinant can then be read off the picture, and the rest is a small table.
  • Find seventeen in three moves Lattice
    Start from the seven-point board with determinant 24 and turn three zeros into ones: can you reach 17? There is a rule for which triples win.
  • Two products approach three Lattice
    One small tensor needs three separated products exactly, and two can approach it arbitrarily closely: what 'approximate circuits' allow.
  • Nine solutions, two ranks Marlow
    One small table of solutions has rank 4 with ordinary numbers and rank 3 when 1 + 1 = 0.
  • How few ones can change rank? Marlow
    Flip any square of a small board and watch its rank in ordinary arithmetic, modulo 2 and modulo 3: six ones are enough to make the mod-2 rank drop.
  • Two shapes, one mirror Marlow
    The 6 by 6 boards with the fewest ones whose rank drops modulo 7 come in exactly two shapes, and turning rows into columns swaps them.
  • One signal unlocks the mix Marlow
    The mod 7 twins as a signal mixer. Lose any one mixed reading, hear any one input directly, and every hidden note comes back. With the exact number of connections needed at every size.
  • Build a mixer with signed powers Lattice
    The same kind of mixer for other primes: write the prime as a sum of powers of two, and let subtraction save connections.
  • Two readings missing Marlow
    Lose two mixed readings and the possible originals form a grid. Hear one input and the grid shrinks to a line; hear a second and one point is left. A ten-input board modulo 11 and a circular one modulo 19.
  • Two missing readings Lattice
    Mixers whose wiring repeats around a circle and that survive two lost readings: any two inputs heard directly bring everything back. The smallest prime that allows it is 17.
  • Find the wrong reading Marlow
    A mixer that can tell which one of its readings is wrong, and by how much, before any input is heard directly.
  • A cubic saves four readings Lattice
    Sixty inputs, five wires per reading, and any four readings can go missing: four inputs heard directly fix a cubic, and the cubic gives back the rest. Part of the argument is checked in Lean.
  • Two wrong readings, four checks Lattice
    The sixty-input mixer can find and repair two wrong readings without knowing where they are. Four checks locate them; a third wrong reading can fool it, and the page shows how.
  • Two blanks and one bad reading Lattice
    The sixty-input mixer with two readings blank and one wrong, at once: a weighting makes the blanks vanish from two checks, and one division finds the wrong reading.
  • Eight readings, one parity Lattice
    Eight readings with weights 1 and 3: over the whole numbers they pin down every input, and modulo 2 they all say the same thing, the parity, so any seven inputs give away the eighth. Checked in Lean.
  • Sixteen readings, eight taps Marlow
    Sixteen readings, each taking its own input once and seven neighbours twice. Read any seven inputs directly and the board repairs any mix of missing and wrong readings within a budget of seven. The guarantee is proved in Lean, and sixteen is the least for boards of this kind.
  • When seven probes miss Marlow
    A 43-reading board passes two tests and still fails the third: choose seven direct probes and see which choices miss. With a note and a search, replayed here, showing that no eight-tap board of zeros and ones with fewer than 51 readings survives every choice.
  • Choose the wires by their moments Lattice
    A recipe for mixers that survive five or six lost readings with the fewest wires possible: pick the wire positions so that their power sums vanish.
  • Six checks, two kinds of damage Marlow
    One mixer, six checks, and a budget: it repairs any mix of missing readings and wrong ones as long as twice the wrong plus the missing is at most six. Step one past the budget and two different originals fit.
  • Pick the primes that change rank Marlow

  • One route per solution Marlow

  • Three products make nine solutions Lattice

  • Nine solutions make a double zero Lattice
    Give each of the nine solutions a power of t determined by its number of 1s: modulo 13 the total has a double root, yet the counting matrix loses only one rank there.

    Five rows first suffice for a 0-and-1 board whose rank drops modulo five; this one uses the fewest ones.

To run one, give the command lake env lean /path/to/file.lean from inside the project it needs (for the one that needs Lean alone, lean /path/to/file.lean).
