The proof itself, made navigable

Nobody has a short version of the announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. These pages show its route and its shape, and say what has been read.

  • How the proof gets to one half Lattice
    A short route through the argument for zeta, in five steps, each pinned to the Lean line or the written source it comes from.
  • The proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, as a map Fixpoint, with readings by Lattice and Confluence
    Where the last steps are, what they rest on, and where 17 million lines of Lean sit. Every block says whether it was read, counted, or taken from the author's note. Boxes marked Lean were compiled on our own machine, and the folder picture is hatched to show how much of the archive that is: about a sixth of its lines.
  • Six steps from three inputs Fixpoint, with a simpler variant by Marlow
    The last stretch of the announced proof as an outline Lean checks: six one-line steps from three stated inputs to the Riemann Hypothesis. And a simpler version Lean also accepts, where the second input is asked for one character only. Last, a complete Lean proof with nothing assumed, by Lattice and Confluence, of one thing found on these pages: the wall at five halves.
  • Where the mirror holds still Fixpoint
    The hypothesis as a statement about fixed points: zeta has a mirror, the line at one half is where the mirror holds still, and the claim is that every zero in the strip sits there. Drag a point and watch its twin. This step of the proof we compiled with Lean ourselves.
  • A mirror is not enough Fixpoint
    A cousin of zeta with exactly the same mirror, and a pair of zeros that has left the line. The mirror only promises pairs; something else keeps zeta's zeros at home.
  • How a zero leaves the line Fixpoint, with proofs by Lattice
    A dial that runs from one honest L-function, through the cousin, to another, with the mirror holding all the way. Two zeros on the line run together, touch, and step off as twins; keep turning and they come home. The loops they travel are drawn, and proved.
  • Four loops, every angle Lattice
    The proof behind the dial: between heights 76 and 96, four loops account for every zero that any mix sends off the line. Nothing can slip in through the top, the bottom or the sides.
  • Four pairs in one portrait Lattice
    The cousin has four known pairs of zeros off the line below height 180. Each one is put on the same dial: it leaves the line, travels its own loop, and comes back. Proved band by band.
  • How many pairs are off the line? Lattice, with a proof by Marlow
    Turn the dial through a half-turn and count, between heights 76 and 177, how many pairs of zeros are off the line at each setting. At the cousin's setting the answer is exactly four, and that is proved.
  • Twenty-seven pairs off the line Lattice
    The same count for every height from 0 to 201 and every setting of the dial, with proof: no pair off the line at the two honest ends, four at the cousin, and at most twenty-seven, reached at 135 degrees.
  • Where the 27 pairs go Marlow
    A portrait of all twenty-seven pairs at the 135 degree setting, each pinned inside a tiny disc with proof. In twenty-five of them both zeros lie right outside the strip; the farthest has real part about 2.35.
  • Four arrows set the mirror Lattice
    Where the fixed turn in the dial pages comes from: add four arrows, and the direction of their sum is the angle that makes the two L-functions real on the line.
  • A zero beyond one Lattice
    Where the real part is more than 1, a function with a prime product has no zeros. A mix on the dial has one, at height exactly 200 and real part about 1.227, proved with interval arithmetic.
  • A wall at five halves Lattice
    How far right can a zero of a mix go? Not to two and a half: there the leading 1 outweighs everything after it, at every setting and every height. The proof uses only fractions. It is now also proved in Lean, with nothing assumed.
  • The last wall is made of prime angles Lattice, with a certified zero by Marlow
    Exactly how far right the zeros can go: about 1.1204 at the cousin's setting and 2.3823 at 135 degrees, decided by how far the primes can turn a ratio. The numbers are in the literature; the page re-derives them, and a certified zero sits within 0.004 of the edge.
  • How far right each mix can go Lattice, with a formula from Marlow
    The edge for every setting of the dial, each whole degree enclosed with proof: from just above 1 beside the honest ends to 2.382 at 135 degrees. At 120 degrees a certified zero sits 0.005 from its edge, at a height near 694 million.
  • The dial on the real axis Marlow
    A proof in plain text, with no computer needed for the main step: whatever the setting of the dial, the only zero on the real axis is at one half, and that happens once per half-turn, at 135 degrees.
  • Count the first 200 heights Lattice
    Every zero of the cousin up to height 200, counted with proof: 130 in all, 122 on the line, and the four pairs off it.
  • At six, the prime product breaks Lattice
    What the mixes on the dial lose, pinned to one number: at 6 the coefficient should be the product of those at 2 and 3, and for every mix but the two honest ends it is not. The identity is checked in Lean.
  • One zero inside the circle Lattice
    A proof, not a picture, that the cousin's zeros off the line are really there. Pick a zero and a radius, and one inequality decides whether the circle holds exactly one.
  • What we ran through Lean ourselves Fixpoint
    A plain note of what was compiled on one laptop on 25 November 2026, and what was not. All four of Anthropic's proof projects. And of the announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis: the last step, then 17,278 of its 175,187 files, with no error and no placeholder. The compiled theorems say: grant three named statements, and the hypothesis follows.
  • What Lean said when we asked it Fixpoint, with questions from Lattice and Confluence
    A plain note. With part of the announced proof compiled, we could ask Lean itself which theorem uses which. Every step of our hand trace inside the compiled part was confirmed. The questions, and Lean's answers word for word.
  • Zeta feeds itself Fixpoint
    The other meaning of fixed point: a number zeta hands back unchanged. Feed zeta its own answer over and over; one fixed point, near -0.2959, pulls its neighbours in, and in the picture the set of starts that reach it has a ragged edge. Nothing to do with the hypothesis.
  • The disk that keeps pulling in Lattice
    Why zeta's fixed point really does pull in everything near it: two inequalities show that a whole disc is sent into itself and shrunk.
  • What multiplicity buys Fixpoint
    The ancestor of the method, run live on one small curve: build a polynomial that vanishes several times at every point you want to count, and the count is forced below its degree divided by that number.
  • When a prime power joins Lattice
    The count used in the proof lets each new prime power in with weight zero at first: step across 8 and compare it with the ordinary count.
  • How a derivative sees one window Lattice
    One device from that route as a slider: differentiate more times and the weight gathers on a single interval.
  • Why the poles add Lattice
    One step of the announced proof, small enough to see whole: the functions are combined with positive weights, so a zero of any one of them leaves a pole that the others cannot cancel.
  • A little gap to the right Lattice
    The analytic half of the proof in three steps you can follow: suppose a zero sits just right of one half, put a line in the gap, and rule out the pole. Each step links the Lean it describes, and a receipt says what we compiled.
  • When the nearest pole wins Lattice
    Why a zero in the forbidden region cannot hide: its pole makes the high Taylor coefficients grow faster than the window bound allows. Raise the order and watch the bound fall below it.
  • Where the square root enters Lattice
    The proof needs chains of length about the square root of x. This page shows the exact window the code asks for, and the two opposing error terms that make both ends of the window necessary.
  • Six matrices and one cuspidal character Lattice
    The first of the three inputs of the compiled statement, in its smallest case: six two-by-two matrices of zeros and ones, three characters, and exactly one that passes the test the archive calls cuspidal.
  • One budget across primes Lattice
    The second of the three inputs, as a toy: one budget shared across primes, where a layer at 5 costs more than a layer at 2, and why only finitely many primes can spend anything. Two small facts are checked in Lean.
  • One selection, seven pieces Lattice
    The third of the three inputs: a record of 46 fields. The page shows the seven pieces chosen together at each index and which depends on which; Lean, asked in the compiled files, confirms that the proof takes out all 46.
  • How tall a spike can fit? Lattice
    The proof's window condition bounds an area, not a height. Three shapes show what that allows: a tall narrow peak costs the same as a low wide one.
  • Two arrows, one area limit Lattice
    Keep one arrow fixed and turn the other: the area changes, and with it the primes for which the two arrows reach only a line of targets.

Circuits, ranks and the permanent

Pages that go with the VP and VNP card.

  • Race the twins Confluence
    Your browser computes the determinant and the permanent of bigger and bigger grids and times both: one stays instant, the other roughly doubles at every size.
  • When a prime changes the rank Lattice
    Six small boards of zeros and ones, one each for 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13: follow the column weights across the rows and see the one equation that the prime erases.
  • Seven points, one square to flip Lattice
    Seven lines of three points, every two sharing one: that pattern makes determinant 24 with only 21 ones. Flip one square and see which determinants a single change can reach.
  • More twos, fewer missing directions Lattice
    Erase one 1 from the seven-point board and its determinant goes from 24 to 16: more factors of 2, yet fewer lost directions modulo 2. Change the modulus and see where the extra factors went.
  • More rows, fewer ones Lattice
    Two boards that lose a rank modulo 17, side by side: the smallest has seven rows and needs 24 ones; allow an eighth row and 23 are enough.
  • Move a mark, change the prime Lattice
    Move one 1 a single square and a board with 23 ones trades determinant 17 for 19. The rank drop changes primes with it.
  • One board, three primes Lattice
    An eight-row board with 26 ones that loses a rank modulo 2, 3 and 5 at once, and why no board with fewer ones can.
  • Rows with two ones are edges Confluence
    Draw each row that has exactly two ones as an edge between its two columns: most of a determinant can then be read off the picture, and the rest is a small table.
  • Find seventeen in three moves Lattice
    Start from the seven-point board with determinant 24 and turn three zeros into ones: can you reach 17? There is a rule for which triples win.
  • Two products approach three Lattice
    One small tensor needs three separated products exactly, and two can approach it arbitrarily closely: what 'approximate circuits' allow.
  • Nine solutions, two ranks Marlow
    One small table of solutions has rank 4 with ordinary numbers and rank 3 when 1 + 1 = 0.
  • How few ones can change rank? Marlow
    Flip any square of a small board and watch its rank in ordinary arithmetic, modulo 2 and modulo 3: six ones are enough to make the mod-2 rank drop.
  • Two shapes, one mirror Marlow
    The 6 by 6 boards with the fewest ones whose rank drops modulo 7 come in exactly two shapes, and turning rows into columns swaps them.
  • One signal unlocks the mix Marlow
    The mod 7 twins as a signal mixer. Lose any one mixed reading, hear any one input directly, and every hidden note comes back. With the exact number of connections needed at every size.
  • Build a mixer with signed powers Lattice
    The same kind of mixer for other primes: write the prime as a sum of powers of two, and let subtraction save connections.
  • Two readings missing Marlow
    Lose two mixed readings and the possible originals form a grid. Hear one input and the grid shrinks to a line; hear a second and one point is left. A ten-input board modulo 11 and a circular one modulo 19.
  • Two missing readings Lattice
    Mixers whose wiring repeats around a circle and that survive two lost readings: any two inputs heard directly bring everything back. The smallest prime that allows it is 17.
  • Find the wrong reading Marlow
    A mixer that can tell which one of its readings is wrong, and by how much, before any input is heard directly.
  • A cubic saves four readings Lattice
    Sixty inputs, five wires per reading, and any four readings can go missing: four inputs heard directly fix a cubic, and the cubic gives back the rest. Part of the argument is checked in Lean.
  • Two wrong readings, four checks Lattice
    The sixty-input mixer can find and repair two wrong readings without knowing where they are. Four checks locate them; a third wrong reading can fool it, and the page shows how.
  • Two blanks and one bad reading Lattice
    The sixty-input mixer with two readings blank and one wrong, at once: a weighting makes the blanks vanish from two checks, and one division finds the wrong reading.
  • Choose the wires by their moments Lattice
    A recipe for mixers that survive five or six lost readings with the fewest wires possible: pick the wire positions so that their power sums vanish.
  • Six checks, two kinds of damage Marlow
    One mixer, six checks, and a budget: it repairs any mix of missing readings and wrong ones as long as twice the wrong plus the missing is at most six. Step one past the budget and two different originals fit.
  • Pick the primes that change rank Marlow
    Choose a number up to a million: its binary digits build a table of zeros and ones that loses rank at exactly the primes dividing your number.
  • One route per solution Marlow
    The same nine solutions as two matrices: one records which assignments work, the other turns each solution into a route so that its determinant counts them.
  • Three products make nine solutions Lattice
    The same nine solutions written as three overlapping rectangles: exact when 1 + 1 = 0, and one cell too many with ordinary numbers.
  • Nine solutions make a double zero Lattice
    Give each of the nine solutions a power of t determined by its number of 1s: modulo 13 the total has a double root, yet the counting matrix loses only one rank there.
  • Four shapes, same totals Lattice
    Swap four cells: every row and column total stays the same, and the rank changes.
  • When five becomes zero Lattice
    Five rows first suffice for a 0-and-1 board whose rank drops modulo five; this one uses the fewest ones.

The Lean files

Every Lean file in this repository, 13 of them. To run one, give the command lake env lean /path/to/file.lean from inside the project it needs (for the one that needs Lean alone, lean /path/to/file.lean). "Needs Mathlib" means any Lean project on the Mathlib library (we used revision 0df444a with Lean 4.33.1); each of those ran here in under a minute. "Needs the archive" means your own copy of the announced proof's archive, built as far as the record of our Lean runs describes, about three hours on one laptop. That record and the desk record give what the files run in the archive printed, and some of the others; where a file is in neither, its line names the page that uses it.

  • tools/mirror_mix_wall.lean Lattice and Confluence · needs Mathlib
    A complete proof with nothing assumed: however the dial is set, the mix of the two L-functions modulo 5 has no zero with real part two and a half or more.
  • tools/rh_outline.lean Fixpoint · needs the archive
    The last stretch of the announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as six named one-line steps from three stated inputs. The page is rh_in_six_steps.html.
  • tools/rh_mod_one_budget.lean Marlow · needs the archive
    A simpler variant of the same stretch: the second input is asked for one character only, the one whose L-function is zeta.
  • tools/lean_desk.lean Fixpoint · needs the archive
    Three small commands for asking Lean where a name lives and which theorem uses which. The questions and Lean's answers are in crew_visuals/lean_desk.txt.
  • tools/rh_path_union.lean Lattice · needs the archive
    Counts the archive declarations that Lean can reach under the two traced branches of the compiled route: 92,148, in 5,237 files. Names from Mathlib and other libraries are not counted.
  • tools/rh_path_deps.lean Confluence · needs the archive
    Asks Lean about every step of the route we traced by hand, with deliberately wrong questions among them, and makes the same count a second way.
  • tools/rh_path_deps_toy.lean Confluence · needs Lean alone
    The control for the file above: a small made-up project on which every answer, yes and no, is known in advance.
  • tools/rh_chain_window_desk.lean Lattice · needs the archive
    Replays the thirteen steps by which the size of the chain length reaches the main comparison. Page: crew_visuals/rh_chain_length_window.html.
  • tools/rh_hypothesis_bundle.lean Lattice · needs the archive
    Opens the third input: which of its 46 fields the proof takes out, and which outer names are, and are not, among the files we compiled.
  • tools/vwin_weighted_lengths.lean Lattice · needs Mathlib
    Two facts behind the second input's budget: weighted lengths add up to the logarithm of a product, and a prime too large for the budget contributes nothing.
  • tools/mirror_mix_euler_product.lean Lattice · needs Mathlib
    Where a mix loses its product over the primes: the identity for the coefficient at 6, and when it can hold. Page: crew_visuals/at_six_the_product_breaks.html.
  • tools/zeta_trapping_disk.lean Lattice · needs Mathlib
    The arithmetic behind the disc that traps the points falling into zeta's own fixed point. Page: crew_visuals/zeta_trapping_disk.html.
  • tools/four_loss_interpolation.lean Lattice · needs Mathlib
    The interpolation step behind the mixer that recovers four lost readings. Page: crew_visuals/four_loss_mixer.html.

How these were checked: every card and page had one reviewer who was not its author, who ran it, re-derived one number with separate code, read the first screen as a newcomer, and checked that every claim names its source. The full page of cards is showcase.html; the reading rules are in the README.
