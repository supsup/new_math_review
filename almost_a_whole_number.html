Almost a whole number

Raise e to the power π√163 and you miss a whole number by less than a trillionth. It is not luck. A theorem about number systems says why, and the archive of the announced proof of the Riemann Hypothesis proves that theorem in Lean. It is one of the results from that archive that Lean has checked on our own machine.

What you are looking at

  1. A grid has a shape, and one function turns the shape into a number. Pick a point τ in the upper half of the plane and mark every point m + nτ, for whole numbers m and n. That is a grid of parallelograms. Two grids have the same shape if one is the other turned and resized. A classical function, called j, gives every shape one number: the same number for the same shape, different numbers for different shapes.
  2. For a tall, thin grid, j is nearly a power of e. Write q for e2πiτ. Then j = 1/q + 744 + 196884 q + 21493760 q² + … with whole numbers all the way. When τ is high up, q is tiny, and everything after the 744 hardly counts.
  3. Some grids come from number systems. The numbers a + b(1 + √−163)/2, with a and b whole, can be added and multiplied and stay in the family. Drawn in the plane they are a grid, with τ = (1 + √−163)/2. For that τ, q is −e−π√163, so 1/q is the number at the top of this page with a minus sign.
  4. The theorem: for such a grid, j is tied to the number system. Look for the smallest equation that j satisfies, with coefficients taken from the number system. Its degree is the class number: the count of different shapes among the system's own sub-grids (its ideals). For √−163 that count is one. Degree one means j is itself a number of the system. The grid is its own mirror image, so j is real, and a real number of this system is a fraction. A separate theorem of the archive says j has no denominator. So j is a whole number: it is −640320³.
  5. Put the two together. eπ√163 = −j + 744 − (a tail that starts 196884 ÷ eπ√163) ≈ 640320³ + 744 − 0.00000000000075.

So the surprise has two parts. The theorem, with those two further steps, makes j a whole number for 7, 11, 19, 43, 67 and 163 alike. Closeness also needs the tail to be small, and the tail shrinks as the number grows: for 7 it is about 47, for 19 about a fifth, for 163 less than a trillionth.

Every such number system, not only the lucky ones

The theorem is not only about class number one. Take any number system made with the square root of a negative number. It has finitely many grid shapes, one for each class of ideals; take j of each. The theorem says those numbers are one another's conjugates: they are exactly the roots of one equation whose degree is the class number, with coefficients in the number system, and no smaller equation has any of them as a root. The separate integrality theorem says the coefficients have no denominators, and because the mirror image of each shape is again one of the shapes, the coefficients are real. So they are ordinary whole numbers. Choose a system and watch them appear.

Each picture is one grid shape, resized so that every cell has the same area. Under it: the numbers a and b that give τ = (−b + √D) / 2a, and j of that shape.

What the Lean statement says, read at the source

The archive names the statement GlobalTheory.Published.HilbertClassFieldJ. We read its definition and the definitions it uses. It is about any number system K of the kind above (a number field of degree two with no real embedding, placed inside the complex numbers) and the grid made by its whole numbers.

IsHilbertClassField K⟮j⟯Adjoining j to K gives the largest extension of K that is abelian and unramified at every prime: the Hilbert class field. The file defines that by its two properties, so nothing is hidden in the name.
finrank K K⟮j⟯ = classNumber KThe degree of j over K is the class number. This is the clause the picture above shows.
j(L) = j(L′) ↔ mk 𝔞 = mk 𝔟Two ideals give the same j exactly when they are in the same class: same shape, same number, and not otherwise.
(idealInvariants K).ncard = classNumber KSo the number of different j values is the class number.
Set.range (fun g ↦ g j) = idealInvariants KThe numbers that the symmetries of K(j) over K send j to are exactly those j values: they are one another's conjugates.

What it leaves out. It does not say j is a whole number, or even that it has no denominator. The second of these the archive proves separately, as a theorem of its own outside its table of published results: GlobalTheory.T.isIntegral_j_of_nonreal_multiplier says that if multiplying by some number that is not real carries a grid into itself, then j of that grid is an algebraic integer. (A second form, GlobalTheory.CMCurves.isIntegral_latticeJ, says the same for a grid carried into itself by the whole numbers of a number system; it is the one our own Lean file uses.) Nor does the statement say which number systems have class number one. That list (ending at 163) is a famous theorem of the last century and is not claimed here; for 163 itself the class number is a short count, which this page does: one shape. And nothing in the statement mentions eπ√163: the series in step 2 is classical analysis, and the page computes its first coefficients itself.

Sources, and what was run

Every number on this page is computed by the page, in whole-number arithmetic carried to 220 decimal places: eπ√n, the shapes of each number system, j of each shape from the two classical series E₄ and E₆, and the equation. A separate program, tools/check_almost_a_whole_number.py (Python, a different formula for j through theta constants, π from Machin's formula), computed the same things, and the two agree on all 77 number systems with discriminant down to −250 and on all 250 values of eπ√n. The first comparison caught a loss of precision in this page at class numbers 14 and 15, since repaired.
