How the proof gets to one half

A short route through Alpöge and Claude’s argument for the zeta function. The big move is to turn a question about zeros into a bound on a count over prime powers.

  1. Ask what a zero would do. Take a weighted sum over prime powers and remove its predictable part. If a zero were to the right of one half, the remainder would exceed a bound close to the square root somewhere in every sufficiently large window of the form below.
  2. Measure divisibility against size. Take one power from each of several distinct primes. If each divides an integer, their product is bounded by its magnitude. The proof applies a related comparison to maps between lattices, or grids of allowed vectors. Divisibility forces their outputs to lie in coarser grids, and size estimates cap the amount of divisibility they can carry.
  3. Repeat the gain. Compose many theta lifts, which are maps carrying automorphic data from one space to another. The construction earns more divisibility with each map, while also having to control denominators, sizes, and how much of the target space the maps cover.
  4. Balance the errors and combine the prime classes. After the comparison is normalized, a cost from the ends has scale X/m and a cost from the steps has scale m. Choose about √X lifts and use auxiliary character estimates to recover the bound for the whole weighted count.
  5. Reflect. The bound rules out zeros to the right. The functional equation reflects a left-hand zero to the right, so every nontrivial zero must be on the center line.

One small divisibility example

Each of 11, 13, 17 and 19 divides the middle binomial coefficient C(20,10) = 184,756, since it occurs in 20! and does not occur in 10!. Their product is 46,189, which fits inside 184,756. Taking logs turns that product into a sum over primes. The proof uses a far more precise size and divisibility comparison for a family of maps. The weighted count and the analytic step

For each prime power n = pk up to X, give it the weight log(p)(X/n − 1). Call the sum S(X) and subtract X log X − (1 + γ)X to get G(X), where γ is Euler’s constant.

The Mellin transform of G has a pole at a zeta zero because it contains the logarithmic derivative −ζ′/ζ. A high-derivative argument rules out a pole to the right of ½ when G has the required one-sided bound throughout arbitrarily large windows [Y, Y3/2]. It is enough to have G(X) ≤ X1/2+ε there for each positive ε. The guide’s analytic step
