When Li’s coefficients make a positive matrix

Take second differences of Li’s coefficients and put the same difference on each diagonal of a matrix. RH makes every weighted sum cTTc nonnegative. A small synthetic quartet can already fail this test using just its first three coefficients.

A matrix is positive semidefinite when every choice of weights gives a nonnegative sum. For the quarter-turn pair the sum is 4((c₀−c₂)²+c₁²). For the off-line quartet, (1, 0, 1) gives −18 even though its first three coefficients are positive. Lean checks both the cosine identity behind the square and the exact negative example. Build the matrix from the coefficients

Set λ₀ = 0 and reflect the sequence so λ−n = λn. The second difference at index k is dk = λk+1−2λk+λk−1. A matrix with entries Tij = di−j is called Toeplitz because its diagonals are constant.

Why RH makes these matrices nonnegative

On the critical line a pair contributes 2(1−cos(nθ)) to λn. Its second difference is 4(1−cos θ)cos(kθ). For any finite list of weights, the quadratic form of that cosine matrix is

4(1−cos θ) · ((Σi ci cos(iθ))² + (Σi ci sin(iθ))²).

This is a nonnegative weight times a sum of two squares. The sum of the weights over all pairs equals 2λ₁, so the sums converge absolutely and each matrix remains positive semidefinite. For a related norm viewpoint on Li coefficients, see Suzuki’s model-space formulation.

Why matrices of every size would imply RH

If every matrix is positive semidefinite, use a vector supported at positions 0 and k with weights (1, 1) or (1, −1). The two resulting inequalities give |dk| ≤ d₀ = 2λ₁. Summing the second differences twice gives |λn| ≤ λ₁n².

The even symmetry of λ and its generating function give log ξ(1/(1−z)) = log ξ(1) + Σn≥1 (λn/n)zn near zero. The quadratic bound makes the series analytic throughout |z| < 1. Exponentiating extends ξ(1/(1−z)) there as a function with no zeros. The disk corresponds to Re s > ½, so reflection rules out nontrivial zeros to the left as well.
